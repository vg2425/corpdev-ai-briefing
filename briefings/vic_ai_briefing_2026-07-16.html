IBM craters 25 percent, its worst day since the 1980s, after warning that AI hardware spending is eating software and mainframe budgets

IBM preannounced weak second quarter results on July 14, sending the stock down more than 25 percent, reportedly its worst single day on record. Management attributed the miss to customers diverting spend away from IBM software, consulting and mainframes toward AI servers and memory chips, with a severe memory shortage forcing buyers to prioritize hardware.

Read analysis ▾

The reported figures put second quarter revenue near 17.2 billion dollars against a consensus around 17.86 billion, with adjusted EPS around 2.93 versus roughly 3.02 expected. CEO Arvind Krishna's framing is the part that matters: clients are spending less on IBM's traditional stack and more on their own AI hardware and memory, and a memory chip shortage that is pushing prices up is forcing customers to fund servers and storage first and defer software, consulting and mainframe purchases. The full earnings report was scheduled for July 22, so these are preliminary numbers.

This is the first large, market moving instance of AI infrastructure spend visibly cannibalizing an incumbent's core business rather than adding to it. The AI capex wave has been narrated as purely additive; IBM is a concrete counterexample where the same budget dollar is moving from legacy software into GPUs, servers and HBM. It also ties the memory shortage directly to enterprise buying behavior, which connects to the SK Hynix and TSMC supply stories running through recent editions.

Skeptic's note: a preannouncement is not the final report, and a 25 percent drop bakes in a lot of narrative that one quarter cannot fully justify. IBM has company specific problems that predate AI, and blaming a broad industry shift is convenient for a management team that missed. Treat the "AI is stealing our budget" explanation as management's characterization until the July 22 detail confirms the mix shift.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

This is a double edged read. The bullish half is that enterprise budget is visibly migrating toward AI compute and memory, which is the exact demand pool CoreWeave monetizes, and IBM is effectively confirming that customers would rather buy AI hardware than legacy software. The cautionary half is the mechanism: a memory shortage severe enough to reorder corporate purchasing is the same HBM scarcity that inflates the bill of materials on every GPU CoreWeave deploys. If component costs are high enough to distort enterprise buying, they are high enough to pressure neocloud unit economics too. Watch memory pricing as a cost input, not just a demand signal.

New York becomes the first US state to pause new hyperscale data centers, freezing permits for up to a year

Governor Kathy Hochul signed an executive order on July 14 creating the nation's first statewide moratorium on new hyperscale data centers, pausing state environmental permits for up to a year while New York writes a data center regulatory framework. The pause applies to new projects expected to draw more than 50 megawatts, and coverage notes four hyperscale facilities already operate in state with applications pending for 39 more.

Read analysis ▾

Hochul framed the move around power and cost, saying these facilities "consume enormous amounts of power, truly threatening to outpace our grid's capacity" and drive up costs for local ratepayers. The order directs the Department of Public Service to develop a Generic Environmental Impact Statement for data centers so future projects meet consistent standards, and the moratorium holds while that work is done, reported as up to a year.

This is the regulatory expression of the power constraint that the EIA and the AI bubble warnings flagged in prior editions. When a large state with meaningful load pauses the single fastest growing category of electricity demand, it converts "grid can't keep up" from a forecast into a permitting reality. It also sets a template other states can copy, which is the real risk: not one moratorium, but a spreading pattern of siting friction in exactly the populous, grid constrained regions where demand is highest.

Skeptic's note: this is a one year pause tied to writing rules, not a ban, and New York was already a hard place to site large loads. The 39 pending applications suggest developers still want in. The honest read is that the binding constraint on the buildout is shifting from chips and capital to power and permits, and that favors whoever already holds interconnection and firm supply.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Siting and power are moving to the center of the competitive map, and this cuts in CoreWeave's favor at the margin. Capacity that is already energized, interconnected and permitted becomes more valuable when new greenfield hyperscale supply gets frozen in major jurisdictions. The flip side is execution risk on any CoreWeave pipeline that runs through states now flirting with moratoria, and rising political scrutiny of ratepayer impact that every large operator will have to answer. The strategic takeaway reinforces the recurring thesis: locked in power and interconnection are the durable moat, and regulatory friction raises the option value of sites secured early.

Nvidia also introduced Cosmos 3 Edge, a roughly 4 billion parameter world model built on Nemotron that runs on edge GPUs and Jetson for on device robot perception and action.

Read analysis ▾

The coalition is the story. Fujitsu, Hitachi, Kawasaki Heavy Industries, FANUC, Kubota, NEC, SoftBank, Sony and Yaskawa are named among partners intending to join the Cosmos coalition and build physical AI on Nvidia's stack, with Kawasaki and Nvidia set to co develop AI robots for shipbuilding. Huang pitched it as a "once in a generation opportunity for Japan" to reinvent manufacturing. This is sovereign AI plus industrial robotics wrapped into one buildout, and it lands the day before Xi Jinping headlines Shanghai's WAIC, so the geopolitical framing is deliberate.

Two distinct demand vectors here. The Vera Rubin AI factory is a concrete, named next generation GPU deployment at national scale, which is a data point on where Nvidia's newest silicon is landing and how fast. Cosmos 3 Edge is the software lever that seeds an entire robotics and industrial ecosystem onto Nvidia hardware, from cloud training down to Jetson at the edge, which is how Nvidia converts a one time factory sale into recurring platform lock in.

Skeptic's note: "world's first national physical AI infrastructure" is Nvidia and partner marketing, and coalitions announced as "intend to join" are softer than signed commitments. The GPU and CPU counts come from the launch materials, not an independent audit. Sovereign AI deals are also a way for Nvidia to book demand that is politically durable but not necessarily incremental to global capacity. Real, but read the superlatives as positioning.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Two things stand out. First, sovereign and national AI factories are a competing model for where large GPU fleets get built and owned: nation states and their industrial champions increasingly stand up captive capacity rather than renting from neoclouds, which is demand that may never reach a merchant provider. Second, the physical AI and robotics wave that Cosmos 3 seeds is a genuinely new inference workload class, training world models and running embodied agents, that expands the total compute pie CoreWeave can address if it positions for it. The competitive question is whether CoreWeave rides the demand expansion or watches sovereign captives absorb it.

The useful signal is directional, not the letter grades.
