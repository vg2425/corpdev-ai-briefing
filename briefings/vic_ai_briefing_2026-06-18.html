SpaceX agreed to buy Cursor maker Anysphere for $60B in an all stock deal, the largest acquisition of a venture backed startup ever

On June 16 SpaceX filed an SEC Form 8K confirming a $60B all stock acquisition of Anysphere, the company behind the Cursor AI code editor, days after its record IPO. The deal folds the most widely used agentic coding tool into the xAI and SpaceX stack and is meant to close the gap with OpenAI, Anthropic and Google in developer facing AI.

▶

What happened. Coverage across CNBC, Axios, TechCrunch, Yahoo Finance, Quartz and TheStreet aligns on the core facts: an all stock structure, a roughly $60B implied equity value, and an expected close in Q3 2026 subject to regulatory review. The strategic logic is that SpaceX, having absorbed xAI earlier this year, wanted a distribution surface and a developer brand, and Cursor is the one agentic coding product with both. Reporting says xAI and Cursor have been jointly training a model on the Colossus supercomputer, and that model is expected to ship inside both Cursor and Grok Build.

The numbers, flagged. Multiple outlets cite Cursor annualized revenue rising from roughly $100M in early 2025 to more than $4B by June 2026, over one million paying users, and deployment across 64 percent of the Fortune 500. Those are reported figures attributed to the deal coverage and Anysphere, not audited disclosures, so treat the exact magnitudes as directional. What is not in dispute is that Cursor is the category leader in paid agentic coding and that the price tag is unprecedented for a VC backed company.

The skeptical read. A $60B all stock price for a company whose moat is a thin application layer over frontier models from rivals is a bet that distribution and workflow lock in matter more than the underlying model. That is plausible, but Cursor has historically depended on Anthropic and OpenAI models, and pulling it onto xAI infrastructure risks degrading the product if Grok class models lag. The deal also concentrates yet another strategic AI asset under Musk, which invites both regulatory and customer concentration questions.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

This is a demand side signal more than a competitive one. Cursor is one of the largest single drivers of inference and code execution traffic in the agentic coding category, and an owner with its own Colossus capacity means that traffic likely consolidates onto xAI and SpaceX infrastructure rather than neutral neocloud capacity. The read through for CoreWeave is twofold: first, the largest model consumers are increasingly vertically integrating their own compute, which is the structural pressure on every merchant GPU provider; second, the deal validates that agentic coding is now a multibillion dollar workload category, and the labs CoreWeave does serve, principally Anthropic and OpenAI, are the ones that lose a flagship distribution channel here, which could push them to invest more in their own first party coding products and the inference capacity behind them. Watch whether Cursor migrates inference off third party clouds, because that is the part that actually touches utilization.

🔥 High Signal Infrastructure Research 9+ sources

NVIDIA Blackwell swept MLPerf Training 6.0, and CoreWeave posted the fastest time to train DeepSeek V3 671B at 8,192 GPU scale

MLCommons released MLPerf Training v6.0 around June 16 to 17, with NVIDIA Blackwell winning every benchmark on both total and per accelerator performance. In the results, CoreWeave reported the fastest time to train the new DeepSeek V3 671B mixture of experts benchmark, reaching the quality target in about 2.02 minutes on 8,192 GB300 NVL72 GPUs over Spectrum X Ethernet.

▶

What happened. This round added new pretraining benchmarks meant to reflect current model trends, including DeepSeek V3, a 671B parameter mixture of experts model that also underpins the DeepSeek R1 reasoning model, and a smaller GPT OSS 20B mixture of experts. NVIDIA was the only platform to submit on every test, swept the fastest time to train results, and reported GB300 NVL72 systems running up to 60 percent faster than GB200 in the same rack scale configuration. The 671B time of roughly 2.02 minutes and the 8,192 GPU scale are figures from the NVIDIA and MLCommons writeups, so treat the precise number as reported rather than independently audited by me.

The CoreWeave angle is unusually direct. The NVIDIA technical blog and HPCwire coverage name CoreWeave as delivering the fastest DeepSeek V3 671B result, alongside Microsoft Azure posting the fastest Llama 3.1 405B time at the same 8,192 GPU scale. That puts CoreWeave at performance parity with the largest hyperscaler in a peer reviewed industry benchmark, on the newest Blackwell Ultra generation, which is a credible third party validation of the fleet rather than a vendor claim.

The skeptical read. MLPerf measures time to a fixed quality target on a controlled task, not real production economics, multi tenant utilization, or cost per token. A top benchmark line does not by itself prove margin or that the same configuration is what customers actually rent. Still, in a market where the differentiator is increasingly "can you stand up tens of thousands of newest generation GPUs and make them train fast and reliably," a verified leading result on GB300 NVL72 is exactly the proof point CoreWeave sells.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

This is close to free, third party validated marketing for the exact thing CoreWeave monetizes: first to market, large scale Blackwell Ultra capacity that trains frontier scale mixture of experts models fast. Being named for the fastest DeepSeek V3 671B time, at the same 8,192 GPU scale as Azure, lets the sales motion point to MLPerf rather than internal benchmarks when underwriting large training contracts, which matters for enterprise and lab customers deciding between neocloud and hyperscaler. It also reinforces the GB300 NVL72 and Spectrum X networking story precisely as the next capex cycle is being negotiated. The honest caveat to carry internally is that benchmark leadership and rental economics are different things, and competitors including Nebius and the hyperscalers submitted competitive Blackwell results too, so this is a lead, not a moat.

🔥 High Signal Infrastructure Agents 9+ sources

GitHub buckled under AI coding agent load, and Microsoft is routing traffic to AWS to keep it inside enterprise SLAs

Microsoft confirmed around June 16 that it is temporarily routing GitHub traffic through Amazon Web Services after a surge in AI coding agent activity pushed availability below the 99.9 percent enterprise SLA. Coverage cites nine service degrading incidents in May and June availability falling toward the high 80s percent, with agents like Cursor, Claude Code and Copilot driving the load.

▶

What happened. The reported picture is that automated agents interact with GitHub fundamentally differently than humans: continuously over the API and command line, never resting, never following the daily and weekly usage curves GitHub capacity planning was built around. The cited stats are dramatic and should be flagged as reported, not confirmed by me: roughly 275 million commits per week putting 2026 on pace for around 14 billion, a step up from about one billion in 2025, and AI agent opened pull requests rising from roughly 4 million in September 2025 to more than 17 million in March 2026. Microsoft is said to describe the AWS arrangement as temporary while GitHub continues migrating to Azure.

One caution on sourcing. Much of this coverage clusters on a handful of trade and aggregator outlets, and the precise availability percentages and commit counts vary between them. The qualitative claim, that agent traffic overwhelmed GitHub and Microsoft is leaning on a rival cloud as a stopgap, is consistent across sources and is the part worth internalizing. The exact figures are not.

The strategic read. This is the clearest public evidence yet that agentic coding is not an incremental load but a different traffic class that breaks assumptions baked into existing infrastructure. The irony that Microsoft, which owns both GitHub and Azure, has to borrow AWS capacity is the headline, but the substance is the demand shape: relentless, API native, weekend insensitive, and growing faster than provisioning cycles.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

This is the demand thesis for the whole sector rendered as an outage. If agent traffic can overwhelm GitHub badly enough that the owner of Azure has to rent a competitor's cloud, then the underlying inference and execution demand from coding agents is running ahead of even hyperscaler provisioning. That is structurally good for anyone selling raw GPU and accelerated compute capacity, including CoreWeave, because the agents behind this load run on inference clusters that someone has to build. The subtler point is about traffic shape: continuous, bursty, API native agent workloads argue for capacity that can be spun up fast and utilized around the clock, which is closer to the neocloud model than to seasonal enterprise planning. The risk to weigh is the same vertical integration pattern as the Cursor story, namely that the largest consumers eventually build their own capacity rather than rent it.

Business Policy 7+ sources

OpenAI's financials leaked ahead of a confidential IPO filing, showing about $34B of 2025 spending against roughly $13B of revenue

Reporting around June 16 to 17, attributed to documents obtained by the Financial Times and writer Ed Zitron, shows OpenAI spent about $34B in 2025 against roughly $13B of revenue. The reported net loss figures vary widely across outlets, and the leak lands just after OpenAI confidentially filed for a US IPO.

▶

What is reported. The spending and revenue figures are fairly consistent across coverage: roughly $34B of 2025 spend, including a reported $19B on research and development and a large payment to Microsoft for cloud and support, against about $13B of revenue. The bottom line number is where sources diverge sharply. Fortune framed it as about $21B in losses, Yahoo Finance and others cited a net loss near $39B, and earlier same day coverage said $38.5B, while at least one account noted that excluding restructuring and non cash items the operating loss was closer to $8B. Because these numbers conflict, treat the net loss as unsettled and the spend and revenue as the more reliable anchors.

Why the spread exists. Net loss for a company like OpenAI is heavily shaped by one time and non cash items, including restructuring and equity related charges, so a single underlying reality can be reported as $8B, $21B or near $39B depending on what is included. That is exactly the kind of figure to source carefully before quoting externally. I would not cite a single net loss number without the methodology attached.

The strategic read. Even on the conservative framing, this is a company spending far more than it earns while preparing a public listing at a reported valuation in the trillions. The investable question is whether revenue growth and the eventual unit economics of inference close the gap fast enough to justify the price. The leak also sharpens the contrast with Anthropic, and the timing, immediately after a confidential filing, will fuel scrutiny of every assumption in the prospectus.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

OpenAI's spending is, in large part, other companies' revenue, and the disclosed scale of its cloud and compute outlays is the clearest public number for how much capital is flowing into AI infrastructure from a single buyer. For CoreWeave the relevant read is that the largest model labs are running enormous, sustained compute bills, which is the demand that underwrites the buildout, but also that those labs are under fresh pressure to show a path to profitability, which over time pushes them toward cheaper, more efficient inference and harder negotiations with every compute vendor. A public OpenAI also means quarterly disclosure of compute spend, which gives the whole sector, CoreWeave included, a cleaner external signal of demand. Watch the IPO risk factors for how OpenAI characterizes compute concentration and cloud dependence, because that language shapes how the market values everyone downstream.

Business Agents 8+ sources

Anthropic opened a Seoul office and announced a wide set of Korean enterprise and government partnerships

On June 17 Anthropic opened its Seoul office, its third in Asia Pacific after Tokyo and Bengaluru, and signed an MOU with Korea's Ministry of Science and ICT on AI safety. It also disclosed enterprise deployments spanning NAVER, Samsung SDS, LG CNS, Nexon, Hanwha Solutions and Channel Corp, with the expansion framed against tightening US AI export controls.

▶

What happened. Anthropic's own announcement and coverage from the Korea Times, UPI, Benzinga and others describe a deep Korean push: Claude Code rolling out across NAVER's engineering organization, Claude Cowork and Claude Code across Samsung Electronics via Samsung SDS, Claude across LG Group through LG CNS, and Claude powering Channel Corp's Channel Talk platform. On the public side, the MOU with the Ministry of Science and ICT focuses on Korean language model safety evaluations with the Korea AI Safety Institute and information sharing on AI enabled cyber threats, plus a research tie up with a consortium spanning KAIST, Korea University, Yonsei and POSTECH.

The strategic read. Two threads are worth separating. The first is commercial: Korea's chaebols are large, technically sophisticated buyers, and landing Samsung, LG and NAVER as Claude deployments is meaningful enterprise revenue and a strong reference base in Asia. The second is geopolitical: several outlets explicitly frame the Seoul expansion against US export controls, positioning Anthropic as deepening allied market access while Washington tightens the rules. UPI's framing in particular ties the timing to the export control backdrop, so read the safety MOU as partly a market access and trust building instrument, not only a safety initiative.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Anthropic is one of CoreWeave's most strategic customers, so where Anthropic grows demand, CoreWeave has a stake in the inference and training capacity behind it. A broad Korean enterprise rollout of Claude Code and Claude Cowork is incremental, sticky inference demand, and enterprise deployments with in region data controls, as noted for the Hanwha case via AWS Bedrock, hint that some of this capacity will be served regionally rather than from US data centers. That is a reminder that geographic footprint and data residency are becoming gating factors for who can serve frontier model demand in allied markets, which is relevant to where CoreWeave chooses to build next. Net, more Claude adoption is a tailwind for the demand base CoreWeave serves, with the caveat that in region requirements may favor providers with local presence.

Agents Models 8 sources

Google retired the Gemini CLI for free and individual users today, pushing developers to the closed source Antigravity CLI

As of June 18, Gemini CLI and the Gemini Code Assist IDE extensions stop serving requests for Google AI Pro, Ultra and free individual users, who are steered to the new Antigravity CLI announced at Google I/O. The move drew developer criticism because Antigravity CLI is not open source, unlike the community built Gemini CLI it replaces.

▶

What happened. The deprecation date is today, June 18, and it primarily hits individual and free tier users. Enterprises on Gemini Code Assist Standard or Enterprise licenses, and GitHub users on enterprise Google Cloud accounts, are reported to be unaffected, and Gemini CLI remains reachable via paid Gemini and Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform API keys. Antigravity CLI supports agent skills, hooks, subagents and extensions at launch, though Google has said there will not be full one to one feature parity immediately.

The friction. The notable backlash is about open source. Google accepted thousands of merged community pull requests to the open source Gemini CLI over roughly a year, cited that participation as proof of success, then restricted the tool and replaced it for most users with a closed source product. That is a real trust cost with the developer community, and it is consistent with a broader 2026 pattern of agent platforms consolidating into proprietary, enterprise gated runtimes.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

This is less a direct infrastructure story and more a signal about where the agent tooling layer is heading: toward consolidated, vendor controlled runtimes tied to a specific model provider's cloud. For CoreWeave the relevant inference is that each major lab is racing to own the developer entry point, Google with Antigravity, the Cursor and xAI tie up, OpenAI and Anthropic with their own coding products, which concentrates agentic coding demand onto a few platforms and the clouds behind them. CoreWeave does not sell developer tooling, so the practical question is which of these runtimes drive inference onto capacity CoreWeave can serve versus onto first party hyperscaler infrastructure. The trend toward proprietary, cloud tied agent platforms modestly favors the integrated hyperscalers over neutral compute, which is worth tracking.

Policy Business 6 sources

The G7 Évian summit closed with child safety measures but no binding AI rules, as lab heads warned against fragmenting allied AI access

The 52nd G7 summit at Évian concluded June 17 with Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis and Dario Amodei having joined leaders for AI discussions, but the output leaned toward child safety commitments rather than binding governance. On June 18, Amodei, backed by Altman, urged democratic nations not to fragment AI access and standards among allies.

▶

What happened. We flagged the G7 attendance in Monday's edition. The closing picture, per French government summaries and coverage from The Hill and TechPolicy.Press, is that the concrete output centered on adapting AI chatbot behavior for children and accelerating safe deployment, building on a late May agreement among G7 digital ministers on protecting children online. Sweeping governance language was reportedly watered down, with the US opposed to multilateral commitments seen as constraining its industrial lead. OpenAI had signaled in advance it expected voluntary commitments rather than binding rules, and that is roughly what emerged.

The follow on. On June 18 Amodei argued that restricting advanced AI access among allies would weaken cooperation and security, a position Altman endorsed, with leaders including Macron and Modi cited as favoring a cooperative framework that keeps allied countries inside frontier AI access while developing safety standards. Read against the same week's US export control posture, this is the labs pushing back on fragmentation while governments tighten the perimeter. I would treat the specific attributions of who said what as reported rather than verified verbatim.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

CoreWeave has no seat at this table, but the fragmentation debate is directly about the operating environment for compute. If allied access to frontier models and the chips behind them stays cooperative, that supports cross border demand and the case for building capacity in multiple allied jurisdictions. If it fragments, data residency and export rules increasingly dictate where compute must physically sit, which raises the strategic value of geographic footprint and complicates where a provider can serve which customer. The Anthropic Korea expansion in this same edition is the concrete version of that tension. Watch for any G7 follow through touching export controls, data center siting or energy, all of which hit CoreWeave's cost base and addressable demand.
