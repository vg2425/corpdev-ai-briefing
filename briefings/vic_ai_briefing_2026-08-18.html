OpenAI leases an 8 gigawatt Ohio campus and Nvidia guarantees up to 105 billion dollars of the financing

On August 17 OpenAI announced a long term lease at the PORTS Pike Technology Campus in Pike County, Ohio, the site of a Cold War era uranium enrichment plant. SB Energy, a SoftBank Group subsidiary, will build and operate it. Nvidia will backstop financing of up to 105 billion dollars covering the first 4.25 gigawatts of compute capacity, with an option to add roughly 3.75 gigawatts more, for an eventual 8 gigawatts. First operations are targeted for 2028. OpenAI cites roughly 35,000 construction jobs and 2,500 permanent operating positions, and reporting says the site will need at least 10 gigawatts of new generation. Flag: the 105 billion dollar figure is a financing guarantee or credit backstop, not cash invested, and outlets describe the lease term as both 10 years and 20 years, which is an unresolved discrepancy. Flag: the 8 gigawatt total is an option adjusted ceiling, not contracted capacity.

Read analysis ▾

Strip the megawatts away and this is a credit story. Nvidia is not buying the data center. Nvidia is standing behind the debt that funds it, which is the same structural move as the six manager memoranda covered in Thursday's edition, only concrete and with a named counterparty and a named site. The lenders are not underwriting SB Energy's balance sheet or OpenAI's cash flows. They are underwriting Nvidia's willingness to make them whole. That is the mechanism that has been quietly re rating the entire AI infrastructure credit stack for a year, and this is the largest single instance of it so far.

Notice what the structure implies about who bears residual risk. If OpenAI's demand does not materialize on schedule, Nvidia ends up with exposure to a partially built campus and to GPUs it has already sold once. Nvidia has been willing to absorb that because the alternative is a demand curve that stalls on financing rather than on appetite. But the concentration is becoming genuinely uncomfortable: Nvidia is simultaneously the supplier, the credit enhancer, and in several cases an equity holder in the offtaker. Bill Gurley's roundtripping objection is not a fringe view any more, and the credit market repricing in story 07 is the same concern expressed in basis points.

The site selection is the underrated detail. A decommissioned federal enrichment facility comes with existing transmission, existing brownfield permitting posture, and a local political economy that is already reconciled to industrial land use. That is a materially faster path to energized megawatts than a greenfield campus in a county holding hearings. Compare it to OpenAI's Project Camellia in Effingham County, Georgia, announced July 22, where Georgia Power will phase 3.2 gigawatts from 2028 to 2032. Ohio is bigger and reportedly on a similar or earlier clock. Speed to power is the binding constraint, and OpenAI is buying it with site history.

The skeptic's read on timing: 2028 first operations means nothing about 2026 or 2027 revenue for anyone. Announcements of this size are option value on a demand curve, not deliveries. The 10 gigawatts of new generation the site reportedly requires does not exist and has not been contracted, and story 04 is precisely about how often that gap does not close.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Two readings, and both are real. The bullish one: this validates the thesis that frontier labs need dedicated multi gigawatt capacity faster than hyperscalers will provision it, and it validates vendor supported financing as the way that capacity gets funded, which is the exact playbook CoreWeave has run with its GPU and contract backed facilities. Every deal like this makes the asset class more legible to lenders and compresses the cost of debt across the sector, and CoreWeave carried 640 million dollars of net interest expense last quarter, so cost of debt is not a rounding error. The threat read is sharper: OpenAI is going direct. It is signing its own leases with its own developers on its own sites rather than leasing GPU hours from a merchant cloud. Every gigawatt OpenAI self provisions is a gigawatt it does not need to rent. CoreWeave's 104 billion dollar backlog is heavily weighted to a small number of frontier labs and hyperscalers, and the observable trend among exactly those customers is toward owning the physical layer. The question for corp dev is whether CoreWeave's differentiation ends up being speed and operational quality on capacity others cannot stand up, or whether it ends up being a bridge that large customers use until their own campuses energize. Ohio 2028 is when that question starts getting answered.

CNBC reported on August 15 that Anthropic's preliminary revenue for its latest completed quarter exceeded 11.5 billion dollars, against 4.73 billion in the first quarter of 2026 and 787 million in the same quarter a year earlier. Separate reporting puts second quarter revenue at 10.9 billion dollars with an operating profit near 559 million dollars, which would be the company's first profitable quarter. On August 13 Bloomberg reported Anthropic is in early talks to acquire Decart, an Israeli AI infrastructure startup, for roughly 6 billion dollars, which would be its largest acquisition. Decart builds software that makes chips run training and inference more efficiently. Flag: none of the revenue or profit figures are audited or company published; they come from press reporting on internal or investor materials, and the 11.5 billion and 10.9 billion figures may cover different periods or definitions. Flag: the Decart deal is unsigned and may not happen.

Read analysis ▾

Take the growth at face value for a moment. Revenue going from 787 million dollars to more than 11.5 billion dollars in four quarters is not a normal software curve, and coding is the reason. Anthropic's mix is heavily enterprise API and Claude Code, and coding workloads have the property that a single user can consume compute without bound. That is the best possible demand shape for whoever owns the inference capacity.

The profit claim deserves more scrutiny than the revenue claim. Ed Zitron's piece calling it a swindle overstates the case but points at the right thing: an operating profit figure at a company of this shape depends entirely on how compute commitments are accounted for. If prepaid capacity is capitalized and amortized over a contract life rather than expensed as consumed, the reported margin flatters the underlying economics considerably. Anthropic has published nothing, so nobody outside can distinguish a genuine gross margin inflection from an accounting boundary. Treat the direction as credible and the number as unverified.

Decart is the part that should make an infrastructure provider sit up. Anthropic is not buying distribution or talent here. It is buying efficiency: a company whose product is getting more useful tokens out of the same silicon. Decart raised roughly 450 million dollars total including a 300 million dollar round in May 2026 at about 4 billion dollars, with Nvidia among its investors, so 6 billion dollars is a 50 percent step up in three months, which itself tells you how contested efficiency assets have become. The reported strategic rationale is that this lets Anthropic's existing compute absorb more demand.

The skeptic's note on the framing that most coverage adopted, which is that this is Anthropic pre positioning for an IPO against OpenAI's: that is inference, not reporting. Nothing in the Bloomberg piece establishes IPO motivation. What is established is early stage talks and a number.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Anthropic is a named CoreWeave customer, and CoreWeave flagged Anthropic business in the second quarter. So the bullish read is direct: a customer whose revenue is compounding at this rate and which has just crossed into operating profitability is a customer whose ability to sign and honor large multi year commitments has materially improved. Counterparty credit quality inside the backlog just got better, and that is exactly the composition question the market asks about the 104 billion dollars. The threat read is Decart, and it is not small. If Anthropic's strategic response to compute scarcity is to buy the ability to serve more demand per GPU rather than to lease more GPUs, then the growth in Anthropic's revenue stops translating one for one into growth in Anthropic's compute spend. Efficiency gains at the customer are margin compression at the supplier in any market where capacity is priced by the hour. The honest framing is that this is a race between demand elasticity and efficiency: if cheaper tokens unlock proportionally more usage, total compute spend still rises and CoreWeave is fine. Jevons has held so far in this cycle. It is worth watching whether it keeps holding, because the entire merchant cloud thesis rests on it.

The FERC show cause deadline landed on August 17: all six US grid operators had to justify or reform how they connect large loads

On June 18 2026 FERC issued six tailored orders under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act directing every US RTO and ISO, together with their transmission owners, either to show cause why existing large load interconnection tariffs remain just and reasonable or to explain what changes would fix the Commission's concerns. That deadline was August 17 2026. Generation adequacy reports were separately due July 20. FERC explicitly encouraged operators to file proposed tariff revisions under Section 205 instead, noting the advantage of holding the pen and getting a more favorable standard of review. Flag: the underlying orders are from June 18, so the policy is not new; what is new is that the filings were due yesterday. Flag: I could not confirm from public sources which specific operators filed what by the deadline, so treat the contents of any individual filing as unknown rather than reported.

Read analysis ▾

This is the most consequential regulatory clock in the sector and it gets almost no coverage relative to a 105 billion dollar headline, because tariff dockets are boring and gigawatts are not. But interconnection rules determine which announced gigawatts become energized gigawatts, and on what timeline, and at whose cost. Everything in story 01 and story 04 routes through this.

What FERC is actually attacking is the mismatch between a queue designed for generators and a new class of very large, very fast, very concentrated loads. The existing process assumes a load grows predictably with population and industry. A 4 gigawatt campus does not. The specific issues in play are cost allocation, whether large loads should pay for the network upgrades they trigger, curtailability and whether flexible load commitments can substitute for firm capacity, and how to stop speculative requests from clogging the queue.

That last one is where this connects to story 04. If Wood Mackenzie is right that roughly seven of every ten requested megawatts are phantom, then queue integrity reform is not a bureaucratic nicety. It is the difference between a real developer waiting five years behind a wall of duplicate applications and waiting eighteen months. Reform that imposes meaningful deposits, site control requirements, or readiness milestones would disproportionately advantage operators who actually build, and disproportionately hurt those shopping the same project to four utilities.

The realistic timeline is slow. FERC now reviews the filings, comment periods open, protests get filed, and litigation is likely on any operator that proposes something contentious. Nobody should model a 2026 benefit. The skeptic's note is that a show cause order produces filings, and filings produce process, and process in the energy sector produces years. The right way to read August 17 is as the start of a two to three year reform arc, not an event.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Speed to power is the moat, and this is the rulebook that governs it. Bullish: CoreWeave went from roughly 1 gigawatt to 1.5 gigawatts of active power in a quarter and has approximately 4.2 gigawatts contracted as of August 11, which means it is an operator with real projects and real site control. Any reform that raises the bar for entering or holding a place in the queue is a competitive advantage for incumbents who can clear it and a barrier for the wave of first time developers Wood Mackenzie describes. It also potentially shortens the interconnection timelines on CoreWeave's own pipeline. Threat: cost allocation reform can cut the other way. If FERC blesses tariffs that push the full cost of network upgrades onto large loads, and does so on a schedule that hits projects already underwritten at prior assumptions, then the delivered cost per megawatt rises on contracts already signed at fixed prices. That is straightforward margin risk on a business already running a 5 percent adjusted operating margin. Worth tracking which specific RTOs, especially PJM and ERCOT, propose load pays structures, because CoreWeave's geographic footprint determines the exposure.

Wood Mackenzie says grid operators will commit to only about 28 percent of the 1,066 gigawatts requested for US data centers

Wood Mackenzie analysis reported by Bloomberg finds that of roughly 1,066 gigawatts of power requested across US data center interconnection applications, utilities and grid operators are likely to commit to about 28 percent. More than two thirds is characterized as phantom load: duplicative non binding requests filed with multiple utilities at once, and disproportionately large projects from first time developers with no track record of building facilities at this scale. Wood Mackenzie's point is that inflated pipeline figures corrupt utility planning and capital budgeting, and because network upgrade costs flow through rate base, ratepayers absorb the error. Flag: this is a Wood Mackenzie proprietary model using Wood Mackenzie's own definition of what counts as likely to materialize, not official RTO or utility data. Flag on dating: the Bloomberg story ran August 12, and much of what circulated on August 17 is recirculation of the same analysis, so this is adjacent to rather than inside this window.

Read analysis ▾

The number to hold onto is not 28 percent. It is 1,066 gigawatts. For scale, total US data center demand was roughly 42 gigawatts in 2026 against about 23 gigawatts in 2023. The pipeline as filed is something like twenty five times current consumption. Nobody who has looked at this seriously believes the full pipeline is real, which means the interesting question was never whether there is phantom load but how much, and what the discount does to planning.

Two mechanisms produce the inflation and they have different implications. The first is benign duplication: a competent developer with one real project files at four utilities to compare terms and speed, and three of those applications will die by design. That is rational behavior in a market where interconnection timelines are the scarce good, and it inflates the queue without indicating any speculative demand. The second is genuinely speculative: entities with no capital, no offtake, and no operating history filing very large applications on optionality. Wood Mackenzie's observation that first time developers file disproportionately large projects points at the second. The 28 percent figure blends both, and only the second category is evidence of a bubble.

This is also a useful corrective in both directions, which is why I am not filing it as bearish. SemiAnalysis spent a July episode dismantling a Bloomberg claim that half of 2026 US data center capacity was delayed, and found a broken denominator in the underlying report. Aggregate pipeline statistics in this sector are consistently unreliable in whichever direction the author's prior points. A 72 percent phantom rate is a striking headline that also happens to be a modeled estimate from a consultancy that sells power market forecasting.

The load bearing implication is political rather than analytical. Utilities that build transmission against phantom demand recover the cost through rates, and residential bills rising because of data centers is already an election issue in multiple states. That is the channel through which this becomes a real constraint: not because the analysis changes anyone's capex, but because it hands regulators and legislators a quantified argument for making large loads pay up front.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bullish, and fairly clearly so. If roughly seven of every ten requested megawatts never energize, then contracted and energized power is a far scarcer asset than the pipeline implies, and the operators who actually hold it have more pricing power than a naive read of announced capacity would suggest. CoreWeave's approximately 4.2 gigawatts of contracted power and 1.5 gigawatts active look considerably better against a real market of roughly 300 gigawatts of eventual commitments than against a nominal 1,066. It also strengthens the competitive position against the long tail of newly funded neoclouds and crypto miner conversions whose announced gigawatts are disproportionately likely to be in the phantom bucket. The threat read is narrower but worth naming: this analysis is ammunition for the argument that AI power demand is overstated generally, and CoreWeave's equity trades on the credibility of the demand curve. It also raises the odds of exactly the load pays cost allocation outcome flagged in story 03. And the honest caution for corp dev diligence: if 72 percent of the market's announced pipeline is not real, then announced gigawatts in any target's data room deserve the same discount.

Set this against story 02 and you get the cleanest available comparison of two frontier strategies. Anthropic at roughly 11.5 billion dollars a quarter and a small operating profit. OpenAI at roughly 24 billion dollars annualized and a 14 billion dollar loss. OpenAI is larger and losing money at scale by choice, spending on research and on distribution to hold the consumer position. The one thing a confidential S-1 does establish is that the machinery exists and can be activated quickly if conditions allow.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

An OpenAI listing would be the single largest repricing event available to the AI infrastructure complex, and CoreWeave is among the highest beta expressions of that complex in the public market. Bullish: a successful trillion dollar print would validate the terminal value assumptions underneath every long dated compute contract in the sector and would compress the cost of capital across it. A newly public OpenAI managing to a loss trajectory is a materially more price sensitive and more capex disciplined customer than a private OpenAI managing to capability. And a failed or postponed listing would take the whole complex down with it. Either way this is the event on the calendar with the widest distribution of outcomes for CoreWeave's multiple.

Google says it was not trained from scratch but replaced via algorithmic improvements and user feedback, and reports 65.3 percent on DeepSWE v1.1 against 49.0 percent for the prior version. On August 14 Z.ai released GLM-5.3, post trained on a 743 billion parameter base, reported to lead CyberGym at 84.5 percent ahead of Claude Fable 5 at 83.8 percent and GPT-5.6 Sol at 83.6 percent, and to score 28.3 on Terminal-Bench 3.0 against 4.6 for GLM-5.2. Read analysis ▾

The GLM-5.3 result is the one that matters and it is not the coding score. An open weights Chinese model leading a cybersecurity capability benchmark ahead of both Anthropic's and OpenAI's flagships, achieved through post training on an existing base rather than a new pretraining run, is a statement about where the capability frontier is cheap. If a lab can buy that much offensive security capability with post training alone, then the compute barrier to reaching a specific dangerous capability is far lower than the compute barrier to reaching general frontier intelligence. Z.ai staging the weights for two weeks of safety evaluation is an implicit acknowledgment of exactly that, and it is the same move Moonshot made with Kimi K3 and OpenAI made with GPT-5.6-Cyber. Delayed weights are becoming the norm for anything with a cyber profile.

Set it against story 08 in the previous edition and against the frontier agent containment failures in Quick Signals below, and a coherent picture emerges: offensive security capability is arriving faster than the containment practice around it, from multiple labs, in multiple jurisdictions, including in open weights. That is a policy story waiting to happen and it will land on the EU AI Act enforcement machinery that went live August 2.

Gemini 3.7 Flash is a different kind of signal. Three weeks between Flash releases, with Google explicitly saying it did not retrain from scratch, means the release cadence has decoupled from the pretraining cycle. Models are shipping as post training and distillation updates on stable bases. Eighteen confirmed releases from fifteen providers in the first half of August is the pattern, not an anomaly. The economic consequence is that inference is becoming the dominant compute workload and that price per token on coding and agent workloads is falling fast. Introductory pricing at 0.75 dollars per million input tokens on a million token context is aggressive.

Skeptic's note on the benchmarks. DeepSWE, CyberGym, Terminal-Bench and their variants are all self administered by vendors with no standing replication authority, and the specific numbers move with harness configuration, scaffolding, and retry policy. A 16 point jump on a software engineering benchmark is exactly the kind of result that shrinks under independent evaluation. Rank order across labs from vendor reported figures is close to meaningless. What is not meaningless is the price and the cadence, both of which are observable.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

The bullish read is the workload mix shift. If frontier labs are increasingly shipping post trained variants on stable bases rather than launching new pretraining runs, and if the compute center of gravity is moving to inference and to reinforcement learning post training, then demand becomes less lumpy and more continuous. Continuous inference load is a better fit for a merchant cloud's utilization economics than episodic training runs that book a cluster for months and then release it. Falling price per token has historically expanded total token volume faster than it has compressed revenue, which is the Jevons argument again. The threat read has two parts. First, inference is a more competitive and lower margin business than training; it is more price transparent, more latency and geography sensitive, and hyperscalers and specialized inference providers like Baseten and Together compete hard in it. Second, and more strategically, capability arriving through post training rather than pretraining means the customers who need enormous coherent training clusters, which is CoreWeave's structural differentiator, may need them less often. Watch whether the reinforcement learning post training workload grows fast enough to replace pretraining as the source of large contiguous cluster demand, because that is the technical assumption underneath the contracted power buildout.

The AI credit trade is cracking: roughly 195 billion dollars of bonds in the first half, Oracle default insurance near 200 basis points

Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle issued approximately 195 billion dollars of bonds in the first six months of 2026, up roughly 80 percent from about 108 billion dollars across all of 2025. Total AI related debt outstanding is tracked toward roughly 570 billion dollars for 2026, with the marginal dollar moving away from public bond markets toward private credit and off balance sheet vehicles. Credit spreads on several of the largest AI borrowers have reached record levels. Oracle five year credit default swaps are quoted between roughly 196.6 and 212 basis points depending on the source and date, meaning it costs roughly 200,000 dollars a year to insure 10 million dollars of Oracle bonds. Morgan Stanley projects securitized products supplying roughly 150 billion dollars of the data center capital gap through 2028. GPU collateralized loans are reported to price at roughly 8 to 12 percent over three to five year terms. Flag: the 570 billion dollar total, the 150 billion dollar securitization projection and the 8 to 12 percent GPU loan range are analyst estimates using varying definitions of AI related debt. Flag: the CDS quotes are point in time and the two figures cited are from different dates. Flag on dating: this is a continuing story rather than a single event, and the underlying reporting spans late July through August.

Read analysis ▾

Equity and credit are now telling different stories about the same buildout, and credit is usually the one worth listening to. Equity holders in the AI infrastructure complex are paid for upside and can tolerate a wide distribution of outcomes. Bondholders are paid a fixed coupon and get nothing extra if the buildout works, so widening spreads mean fixed income investors are pricing a rising probability that some of this does not work. Two hundred basis points on Oracle, an investment grade issuer, is a meaningful statement.

The composition shift is the more important detail than the total. Bonds are visible, rated, covenanted and marked daily. Private credit and off balance sheet special purpose vehicles are not. When the marginal dollar of financing migrates from the first category to the second, aggregate leverage statistics understate actual leverage and the market loses the ability to see where risk sits. Quinn Emanuel has been publishing on the litigation risk in exactly these structures, which is what lawyers do roughly eighteen months before a cycle turns. The specific hazard they identify, that these structures separate economic risk from operational control, is the same hazard that made 2008 structures hard to unwind.

GPU collateralized lending at 8 to 12 percent deserves its own scrutiny. The collateral is a depreciating asset with a technology obsolescence curve, in a market where Nvidia releases a new architecture annually and where the residual value of an H100 or a GB200 in year four is genuinely unknown. Lenders are underwriting to contract cash flows and to vendor backstops, not really to the hardware. Which means the collateral is the contract, which means the credit is the counterparty, which means the whole structure is a bet on a small number of frontier labs. Story 01 is that bet made explicit.

The skeptic's note cuts against alarmism though. Spreads widening from very tight levels to merely normal levels is not distress. These are among the most cash generative companies in history, and 195 billion dollars of issuance against their combined operating cash flow is aggressive but not obviously reckless. What would change the read is a single failed offering, a covenant breach at a large neocloud, or securitization deals failing to place. None of that has happened.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

This is the most direct read through in the edition, because CoreWeave is a levered infrastructure business whose equity story depends on cost of debt. Net interest expense was 640 million dollars in the second quarter against 2.575 billion dollars of revenue, roughly a quarter of the top line. That is the sensitivity. Bullish: the maturing of GPU and contract backed securitization into a recognized asset class, with Morgan Stanley sizing it at 150 billion dollars through 2028, means a deeper and eventually cheaper funding market for exactly the instruments CoreWeave issues. Nvidia backstops of the kind in story 01 do the same thing by making the paper more legible to insurers and pensions. Threat, and it is the live one: CoreWeave sits structurally wider than Oracle on the credit curve, and if investment grade AI borrowers are being repriced then high yield AI infrastructure gets repriced harder and sooner. SemiAnalysis devoted a chapter of its July 23 episode specifically to CoreWeave credit spreads, which tells you where the sophisticated money is looking. A rising cost of incremental debt does not just compress margin, it changes which projects clear the return hurdle, which slows the contracted power conversion that the whole backlog narrative depends on. If there is one number to track between editions, it is CoreWeave's own spread rather than its share price.

⚡ Medium AI Infrastructure & Chips Policy 7+ sources
The memory supercycle turns into an antitrust problem, and DDR5 is now several hundred percent more expensive than a year ago

Reporting on August 18 puts DDR5 module prices up somewhere between roughly 355 percent and 933 percent over twelve months depending on the part, as Samsung, SK hynix and Micron redirect wafer capacity to high bandwidth memory for AI accelerators. A July 2026 Bloomberg report put DRAM spot prices up nearly 700 percent year over year. SK hynix has said its HBM, DRAM and NAND capacity is essentially sold out for 2026 and has warned the shortage could persist past 2030; Micron has exited consumer memory entirely. The three manufacturers now face a US antitrust class action alleging price manipulation through the HBM transition. Samsung's semiconductor division reported 53.7 trillion won of operating profit in the first quarter of 2026, roughly 36.1 billion dollars and about 94 percent of company profit. Flag: the price increase ranges vary enormously by part and by source, and the 933 percent upper bound is a specific SKU rather than a market average. Flag: the antitrust action is a filed complaint with allegations, not a finding. Flag on dating: the shortage is a running story from late 2025 onward; what is incrementally new is the antitrust filing and the consumer price documentation.

Read analysis ▾

The mechanism is simple and worth stating plainly because it explains a lot of otherwise confusing datapoints across the sector. HBM and conventional DRAM compete for the same wafer starts. HBM carries much higher margin. So capacity moves to HBM, conventional DRAM supply contracts, and prices for everything from server memory to laptop RAM rise. This is not a shortage in the sense of a supply disruption. It is a deliberate allocation decision by three firms that between them control effectively all of the supply.

Which is exactly what makes the antitrust claim non frivolous even if it is unlikely to succeed. An oligopoly of three, all reallocating capacity in the same direction at the same time, producing a seven fold price increase, in an industry with prior price fixing convictions, is a fact pattern plaintiffs' lawyers were always going to bring. The defense is straightforward and probably correct: this is rational independent margin maximization, not coordination. But discovery in a case like this produces documents, and documents in semiconductor pricing cases have historically been bad for defendants.

The number with the most forward information is SK hynix warning the shortage could extend past 2030 and customers reserving supply years ahead. If that holds, memory is not a cyclical cost spike to be absorbed but a structural repricing of a major bill of materials line. Microsoft already attributed roughly 25 billion dollars of its approximately 190 billion dollar 2026 capex plan to component price inflation rather than additional capacity. That is the honest way to read hyperscaler capex growth right now: some meaningful fraction of it buys nothing extra.

Skeptic's note. Memory is the most reliably cyclical market in semiconductors and every supercycle in its history has been declared structural at the peak. Capacity is being added, and when it lands the correction is usually violent. The late July memory and HBM selloff in equities was the market testing that thesis. Anyone underwriting a five year contract on the assumption that memory stays at these prices is taking a view, not making a base case.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

This is a cost of goods problem with a contract structure wrinkle, and it is the least discussed risk in the CoreWeave story. Memory is a large and rising share of the cost of an AI server, HBM most of all, and if memory prices stay where they are then the capital cost per delivered megawatt keeps climbing. For a business that signs multi year fixed price capacity contracts and then buys the hardware to serve them, input cost inflation between signing and provisioning lands directly on gross margin. The second quarter's compression from a 16 percent adjusted operating margin a year ago to 5 percent has several causes, but rising hardware cost per unit of capacity is plausibly among them, and it is worth pressing on in diligence. The bullish counterweight is real though: a genuine structural memory shortage with capacity sold out through 2026 is a barrier to entry that hurts new entrants far more than incumbents. A neocloud trying to stand up its first gigawatt now pays materially more per server than CoreWeave paid for capacity it has already deployed, and may simply not be able to get allocation. Scarcity of HBM protects the installed base. It also means that any target CoreWeave evaluates whose model assumes normalized memory pricing is running an optimistic case.
