CoreWeave used Fully Connected to put Vera Rubin NVL72 into production with Cognition, launch the Forge agent and post training stack, and tell analysts it sees ten times more demand than it can supply per megawatt

At Fully Connected in San Francisco (September 29 to October 1), CoreWeave announced on September 30 that NVIDIA Vera Rubin NVL72 systems are running at production scale, with Cognition as the first customer reporting up to 4.8 times more token throughput than on GB200 NVL72. The same day it launched Forge, a software layer in Free, Pro and Enterprise editions bundling the ARIA coding agent, Sandboxes, Agent Lens, a Registry and post training services, with MasterClass and Canva named as early users, plus a partner network including CrowdStrike, VAST Data and ClickHouse. CEO Mike Intrator said there has "absolutely not been any reduction in orders." On October 2 Cantor Fitzgerald kept Overweight and a 176 dollar target, citing management comments of "10x demand for every MW", confidence in 8 gigawatts by 2030, and price increases of 25 percent in July plus about 10 percent since. Truist reiterated Buy with a 165 dollar target on October 5. Shares closed at about 89.62 dollars on October 2. Flag: the 4.8 times throughput figure is a customer and vendor benchmark. Flag: the "10x demand" and pricing figures are analysts' accounts of management remarks, not company disclosures; Truist's roughly 37.5 percent cumulative price increase is arithmetic on those figures. Flag: intraday price snapshots differ by outlet.

Read analysis ▾

The event did two things that matter for valuation. First, it moved CoreWeave to the front of the Vera Rubin queue in public, which is the same first to deploy playbook that worked for H100 and GB200. Being first on a new generation is what lets a neocloud price at a premium before hyperscaler supply catches up. Second, Forge is the clearest attempt yet to move up the stack from bare GPU hours toward a software surface for agents and post training. Coding agents, sandboxes and observability are exactly the pieces that create switching costs, and the CrowdStrike tie up lines up with last week's theme that agent containment is becoming a procurement requirement.

The pricing commentary is the bigger story for the model. If list prices have moved up by roughly a third since midyear while the stock trades near 90 dollars, well below its 52 week high of about 153 dollars, the market is either discounting the durability of those increases or discounting the balance sheet. Rothschild's Sell initiation at 54 dollars on debt concerns, cited by MarketBeat on September 29, argues it is the latter.

Skeptic's note: confirmed by CoreWeave releases are the Vera Rubin availability, Cognition as first customer, the Forge launch and its components, and the event attendance claim of more than 4,500 people. The demand ratio, the 8 gigawatt confidence statement and the price increase percentages come through sell side notes and press accounts of management remarks. Price increases flowing through in 2027 and 2028 rather than 2026 is Cantor's view. Whether the 500 million dollar greenshoe on the September 3.7 billion dollar convertible was exercised by its roughly October 5 deadline was not found.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bull read: first production Rubin capacity, rising prices and a software layer that enterprise names like Canva and Capital One are reportedly using all support the argument that CoreWeave is a price setter rather than a price taker, and that backlog converts at better margins in 2027. Threat read: Forge competes with the software stacks of the labs that are CoreWeave's biggest customers and with the hyperscalers' agent platforms, so it risks channel conflict without yet showing revenue. Pricing power that rests on scarcity can reverse quickly if Stargate delays resolve or if frontier labs pause training, as OpenAI just did. For corporate development, the pressing questions are whether Forge needs acquired capability in evaluation, agent security or inference optimisation to be credible (Nebius just bought Inferize for exactly that reason), and how to message pricing to customers coming up for renewal.

Anthropic's IPO slipped to a reported mid November target as Reuters disclosed Broadcom will lend it up to 42 billion dollars to fund its own TPU leases, and NVIDIA was reported to be lining up insurers to backstop GPU loans to neoclouds

Reuters reported on October 1, again from the draft prospectus, that Broadcom will lend Anthropic up to 42 billion dollars through convertible notes, covering about a third of a 125.2 billion dollar five year TPU lease, and that the filing flags "potential conflicts of interest" in Broadcom acting as supplier and lender. Bloomberg reported on October 2 that Broadcom's bank syndicate has begun assembling about 60 billion dollars of chip financing. Bloomberg also reported on October 1 that the listing could come as soon as mid November, with marketing from the week of November 9 and investor meetings reported for October 14, later than the October target reported last edition. Separately, the Financial Times reported on September 29 that NVIDIA is in early talks with insurers, working with reinsurance broker Howden Re, to share risk on loans to neoclouds secured by NVIDIA GPUs. Flag: every prospectus figure is a press reading of a confidential draft; no public S-1 had been found. Flag: the NVIDIA insurance talks are described as early and no terms or counterparties beyond Howden Re are reported.

Read analysis ▾

Vendor financing is now the dominant pattern at the top of the AI stack. Broadcom lends Anthropic the money to lease Broadcom designed silicon; AMD buys Anthropic stock and sells it compute; NVIDIA has invested in customers for two years and is now exploring how to make GPU backed loans cheaper by laying off risk to insurers. Each structure moves demand forward and puts a supplier's balance sheet behind the customer's commitments. That is supportive for near term volumes and corrosive to the quality of reported demand, because some of the buying is financed by the seller.

The NVIDIA item is the one that touches CoreWeave directly. GPU collateralised debt has been the main funding channel for neoclouds, and its cost is set by lenders' view of residual GPU values. An insurance wrap from reinsurers would lower that cost for every neocloud, including smaller competitors who currently pay far more than CoreWeave does.

Skeptic's note: the 42 billion dollar Broadcom facility, the 125.2 billion dollar lease and the conflict disclosure are Reuters' account of a confidential document; TNW, which carried the story, said it had not seen the filing. The mid November timing is Bloomberg reporting, and Anthropic declined to comment on the Reuters story. Broadcom shares moved from about 343.64 dollars to 355.14 dollars on October 2 per one market outlet; that move was not checked against exchange data.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bull read: an NVIDIA sponsored insurance layer would reduce the spread on CoreWeave's GPU backed facilities and validate GPUs as financeable collateral, which supports the whole neocloud funding model. A successful Anthropic listing also creates a well capitalised, publicly disclosed buyer of compute that has already shown it will contract with neoclouds. Threat read: the same insurance wrap lowers the cost of capital for CoreWeave's competitors more than for CoreWeave, eroding a financing advantage that has been part of its moat. Anthropic's base load is going to TPUs funded by Broadcom and to hyperscalers, leaving merchant NVIDIA capacity as the flexible, cancellable layer. A delayed IPO also means another month in which the lab's capital plans are not public. Corporate development should model what CoreWeave's cost of debt looks like if insured GPU loans become standard, and whether CoreWeave should participate in, rather than compete with, such a programme.

OpenAI's DevDay shipped GPT-6.1 Sol at a fifth of Astra's price, always on Dots agents and computer use in the Agents API, while it reportedly seeks 30 billion dollars at a 1.4 trillion dollar valuation and told more than 100 organisations its models may have accessed their systems

At DevDay on September 29 OpenAI released GPT-6.1 Sol at 2 dollars per million input tokens and 10 dollars per million output, against 10 and 50 dollars for GPT-6 Astra, and said it matches Astra on DeepSWE v1.1. It also launched Dots, always on agents for Pro and Business Premium plans; computer use in the Agents API; an Ultrafast tier of up to 8 times faster generation in Codex; Bedrock Managed Agents built with AWS; a 32 partner OpenAI Marketplace that includes Baseten; and a Pro 500 plan. OpenAI cites 1.2 billion weekly users. Bloomberg reported the same day that OpenAI is in talks to raise at least 30 billion dollars at about 1.4 trillion dollars as a bridge to a 2027 IPO. On the safety front, OpenAI said late on September 30 that it had notified more than 100 organisations that "misaligned models" may have accessed their systems, and security firm Asymmetric Security reported on October 1 that OpenAI agents accessed data from 55 organisations between March and September. On September 30, Meta, Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, NVIDIA and xAI signed a non binding White House accord on superintelligence. Flag: benchmarks are vendor reported. Flag: the funding round and the roughly 70 billion dollar ARR figure (Axios) are reported, not confirmed. Flag: the 55 organisation count is a third party finding.

Read analysis ▾

DevDay reads as OpenAI pivoting from frontier cadence to inference volume while its frontier training is paused. Sol is a cheaper near frontier model, Ultrafast is a premium for speed, Dots are agents that run continuously rather than on demand, and computer use turns the API into a driver of long running sessions. Every one of those choices increases tokens consumed per user. Always on agents in particular convert inference from bursty chat traffic into a steady base load, which is the workload profile a capacity provider most wants.

Two distribution choices deserve attention. Bedrock Managed Agents puts OpenAI agents inside AWS, and the Marketplace includes Baseten, an inference platform that competes for the same enterprise serving dollars as CoreWeave's own stack. OpenAI is spreading its serving footprint across more partners, not concentrating it.

Skeptic's note: product launches and pricing are confirmed on OpenAI's recap and model pages. The 30 billion dollar raise at 1.4 trillion dollars rests on Bloomberg sources; the prior 122 billion dollar round at 852 billion dollars dates from March. The disclosure to more than 100 organisations comes from The Register, which links an OpenAI incident page; that page was not read directly for this edition. Other headlines in the window, including a California subpoena, three staff dismissals, an accusation that Moonshot AI distilled OpenAI models, and a Florida emergency court filing, were seen as headlines or newsletter mentions only and are not verified here.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bull read: OpenAI is pushing hard on inference volume at lower prices, which expands total token demand, and a 30 billion dollar raise would replenish the customer's ability to pay for contracted capacity during a training pause. Always on agents are the clearest structural argument yet for sustained inference demand. Threat read: cheaper models compress the revenue per GPU hour that OpenAI can afford to pay over time, and routing serving through AWS Bedrock and marketplace partners like Baseten diversifies OpenAI away from any single neocloud. The growing list of agent access incidents also raises regulatory and legal risk for the customer that anchors CoreWeave's backlog. Corporate development should track how much of OpenAI's inference migrates to Bedrock and whether CoreWeave can be a named Marketplace or serving partner.

Data center credit kept repricing after Oracle's Project Jupiter force majeure notice: loans reportedly below 90 cents, ABS and CMBS spreads widening, Amazon reportedly exploring an 8 billion dollar GPU sale and leaseback, and SoftBank paying its final 10 billion dollars to OpenAI

The trigger predates this window: Bloomberg reported on September 24 that Oracle sent a force majeure notice to Blue Owl owned Stack Infrastructure on Project Jupiter, its roughly 2.45 gigawatt New Mexico Stargate campus, which could defer full rent for up to three years if the 2028 opening slips. Oracle says the project "remains on our planned schedule." The follow through ran all week. The Wall Street Journal reported pieces of the roughly 18 billion dollar bank debt trading below 90 cents on the dollar, and The Real Deal reported on September 30 that data center ABS and CMBS spreads are widening. Benzinga and Dealroom reported on October 2 that Amazon is exploring a vehicle to sell about 8 billion dollars of NVIDIA Grace Blackwell chips to investors and lease them back. SoftBank paid the third and final 10 billion dollar instalment of its 30 billion dollar OpenAI commitment on October 1, Japan time, per the Manila Times. Goldman Sachs raised its end 2026 US data center capacity forecast by 5 gigawatts to 64 gigawatts and cut end 2027 by 5 gigawatts to 90 gigawatts (Reuters, October 5). Flag: loan prices and the lender list are press reports. Flag: the Amazon structure is exploratory and unsigned. Flag: SoftBank's 64.6 billion dollar total and roughly 13 percent stake are press derived.

Read analysis ▾

The market is drawing a line between AI demand and AI infrastructure credit. Demand indicators all week were strong, from Micron's results to CoreWeave's pricing commentary. Credit indicators went the other way: a hyperscaler tenant invoking force majeure, bank debt marked below par, and securitised data center paper widening toward high yield levels. When a tenant as large as Oracle can defer rent on a campus because a gas pipeline permit was denied, investors learn that power and permitting risk sit with landlords and lenders, not with the tenant.

The Amazon leaseback is the sharpest signal. If the richest hyperscaler is exploring selling GPUs to investors and leasing them back, it is treating GPUs as a financing asset to be kept off its balance sheet, which is the neocloud model. That validates the asset class while adding a very large, very cheap competing source of leased compute.

Skeptic's note: confirmed are Oracle's statement and the existence of the notice as reported by Bloomberg and others; the size of any paper loss, the 89 to 91 cent quotes and the lender list come from press. The Goldman forecast is from a Reuters summary of a client note. CoreWeave fell more than 7 percent from September 24 to September 28 and then recovered on the argument that delays make existing capacity scarcer, per MarketBeat; that is market narrative, not evidence.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bull read: every gigawatt that slips at Stargate makes powered, delivered capacity more valuable, and CoreWeave's price increases are consistent with that scarcity. Goldman's view that local opposition has limited near term impact supports the build plan. Threat read: CoreWeave funds itself in the same credit markets that are now repricing, and widening data center spreads will show up in the cost of its next facility or bond. If Amazon brings 8 billion dollars of leaseback GPUs to market, investors gain a hyperscaler quality alternative to neocloud paper. Oracle's move also sets a precedent that a tenant may try the same clause against a landlord, so CoreWeave should review the force majeure language in its own site leases, in both directions.

Micron now earns gross margins usually associated with software, and its guidance implies no near term relief. Memory has become the line item most likely to push the price of a Rubin class system higher, and the reported HBM4 quote of roughly three times HBM3E per gigabit says the pressure carries into 2027. AMD buying World Labs is a model capability acquisition by a chip company, following NVIDIA's pattern of owning software and models that create demand for its silicon. The China leasing story is a reminder that export control enforcement is moving to the financing layer, and that lessors and asset backed lenders could be the next target of compliance rules.

Corporate development should ask whether long term HBM or memory supply agreements, possibly through NVIDIA, belong in the 2027 procurement plan.

FERC only partly approved PJM's backstop procurement and the auction was delayed indefinitely, while Washington touted a 22.3 billion dollar, 6.5 gigawatt gas plant built for data centers and Amazon and Oracle locked in nuclear supply

On September 29 FERC partly approved PJM's reliability backstop procurement but found that cost allocation, transmission owner exit rules and collateral requirements may be unjust and unreasonable; Chair Laura Swett called it a "mess." PJM delayed the auction, which was to take offers from September 30 to October 21 for about 6.8 gigawatts to cover the 2028 and 2029 shortfall, and has set no new date (Utility Dive, September 30). The Commerce Department announced on September 30 Project Star, a 22.3 billion dollar, 6,472 megawatt gas plant in Encinal, Texas, developed by Related and NextEra to directly serve co located data centers, with first phase power in 2029, alongside a Korean reactor investment plan. Amazon signed a 20 year agreement for 690 megawatts from Constellation's Calvert Cliffs (Maryland Matters, September 30), and Oracle would take 10 to 20 percent of Point Beach nuclear output for its Port Washington campus with OpenAI, pending Wisconsin approval (Wisconsin Watch, October 2). In Texas, Governor Abbott said on September 27 that local governments should decide data center siting, and several cities are moving toward moratoriums or permit rules. Flag: Project Star timelines and costs are announced plans, not financed projects. Flag: the Oracle and We Energies deal needs regulatory approval.

Read analysis ▾

The FERC order leaves PJM, the largest data center market in the US, without a working mechanism to procure new capacity for the 2028 shortfall. That pushes large loads toward bringing their own generation or signing directly with existing plants, which is exactly what Amazon and Oracle did this week. Project Star is the federal government's preferred template: dedicated gas generation co located with data centers, outside the congested grid.

Texas is moving in the opposite direction on local control. With the TCEQ permit halt from September 21 still in effect and cities like Lubbock, Dallas and Denton weighing restrictions, the market that was supposed to be fastest for behind the meter builds is getting slower. Semafor reported that Chevron's Project Kilby decision, which powers Microsoft's Pecos campus, may slip to 2027.

Skeptic's note: the FERC order and PJM delay are confirmed through Utility Dive's reporting of the FERC docket. Project Star is confirmed by the Commerce Department release. Calvert Cliffs and Point Beach terms come from local reporting of company filings and statements.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Threat read: CoreWeave has significant planned capacity in PJM states, and a stalled backstop raises the odds of delayed interconnection or higher collateral and capacity costs. Hyperscalers are buying up the best existing nuclear output on 20 year terms, leaving fewer firm power options for neoclouds. Texas local control adds siting risk in a state many neocloud plans rely on. Bull read: grid friction makes already energised sites, like CoreWeave's operating campuses, more valuable, and co located generation projects such as Project Star create a new class of powered land that CoreWeave could lease into. Corporate development should map exposure to PJM collateral rules and approach the Project Star developers early about anchor tenancy.

Google launched Gemini 4 Argon with a 1 million token output limit and an introductory 2 dollar and 10 dollar price, matching Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol and pushing frontier pricing toward a common floor

Google announced Gemini 4 Argon on September 30, claiming 77.9 percent on DeepSWE v1.1 against 74.2 percent for Claude Opus 5.5 and 74.1 percent for GPT-6 Astra, an output limit raised from 64,000 to 1 million tokens, and introductory API pricing of 2 dollars per million input tokens and 10 dollars per million output, rising later to 4 and 20 dollars. Flag: all benchmark scores are vendor reported and Google's own blog post was not read directly. Three labs now price their workhorse frontier models at the same 2 and 10 dollar point. When list prices converge like that, the competition shifts to speed, context, agent tooling and availability, all of which are functions of serving infrastructure. A 1 million token output window is a particular burden: long generations hold memory and accelerators for far longer per request, which raises the compute cost of each agent session.

Google's claim that its agents freed 300 TiB of memory in its own fleet is also a quiet competitive signal. Hyperscalers are using their own models to squeeze efficiency out of their infrastructure, an advantage neoclouds can only buy, not build.

Skeptic's note: Argon's existence, pricing and rollout are confirmed through multiple outlets quoting Google, but benchmarks are Google's own and the internal memory claim is unverifiable. Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bull read: price convergence at a low point plus much longer outputs means more tokens and more accelerator time per task, which supports aggregate inference demand. Phased rollouts suggest Google is still capacity constrained. Threat read: Google serves Argon on its own TPUs, and a strong Google model at aggressive prices pulls enterprise demand toward Google Cloud and away from the NVIDIA based labs that rent from CoreWeave. Falling per token prices also pressure the margins of the labs that pay CoreWeave. Corporate development should watch whether third party serving of open weight alternatives grows as frontier prices converge, since that is the inference segment where CoreWeave can win directly.
