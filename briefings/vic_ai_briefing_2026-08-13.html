CoreWeave beat, raised, and printed a 104 billion dollar backlog, and the market added roughly a fifth to the market cap in a day

Second quarter revenue was 2.575 billion dollars against 1.212 billion a year earlier, growth of 112 percent. GAAP net loss per share was 1.14 dollars, versus consensus nearer a 1.41 dollar loss. Revenue backlog was approximately 104 billion dollars at June 30, which the company says excludes more than 25 billion dollars of net new customer commitments added in early third quarter. Active power grew by nearly 500 megawatts to 1.5 gigawatts, and contracted power reached approximately 3.7 gigawatts. Management raised full year 2026 revenue guidance to a range of 12.4 to 13.2 billion dollars with adjusted operating income of 960 million to 1.15 billion dollars, guided third quarter revenue to 3.4 to 3.6 billion dollars, and lifted the year end active power target above 1.85 gigawatts. The stock closed up 19.50 percent on Wednesday August 12. Flag: the revenue, loss, backlog and power figures are from the company press release and are therefore first party but audited only at year end; the guidance is guidance.

Read analysis ▾

Monday's edition argued that the four numbers that mattered were margin trajectory, backlog composition, customer concentration and cost of debt. Three of the four came in constructively and one did not.

Margin first, because it is where the equity story lives. Adjusted operating income was 128 million dollars on a 5 percent adjusted operating margin, against 200 million dollars and 16 percent a year ago. That is margin compression, not expansion, on a base that more than doubled. GAAP operating income was actually negative 49 million dollars, a minus 2 percent operating margin, against a positive 2 percent last year. Adjusted EBITDA of 1.51 billion dollars on a 59 percent margin looks robust until you set it against 640 million dollars of net interest expense and the depreciation that adjusted EBITDA excludes, at which point the net loss of 626 million dollars is the honest number. Michael Intrator called the quarter an inflection point where scale began to translate into operating leverage. The reported second quarter margins do not yet show that. The full year adjusted operating income guide of 960 million to 1.15 billion dollars on 12.4 to 13.2 billion of revenue implies roughly 8 percent for the year, which against 5 percent in the second quarter means the second half has to carry a materially better margin than the first. That is the load bearing assumption in the raise, and it is a forecast.

Backlog is the genuinely strong datapoint. Approximately 104 billion dollars, up around 246 percent year over year, with more than 25 billion dollars of additional net new commitments landed in early third quarter and explicitly excluded from the figure. Read the footnote though: backlog is defined as remaining performance obligations plus other amounts the company estimates will be recognized under committed contracts, subject to delivery and availability of service. That second clause is doing real work. A portion of this number is an estimate conditioned on CoreWeave successfully energizing capacity it has not yet energized.

On concentration, the disclosure moved in the right direction without resolving the criticism. New logos named include Bentley Systems, Caterpillar, Grammarly, Isomorphic Labs and Sunday Robotics, and expanded relationships include Cognition, Databricks, Hudson River Trading, Periodic Labs, Rescale and Runway ML. That is a broader and more industrial mix than a year ago. But the largest contracts remain the largest contracts, and the release does not restate customer concentration percentages. Enterprise logos diversify the story faster than they diversify the revenue.

Cost of debt is the item that did not improve. Net interest expense of 640 million dollars is up from 267 million a year ago and now runs at roughly a quarter of revenue. Interest expense grew faster than revenue. On a business whose adjusted operating income was 128 million dollars in the quarter, the financing line is roughly five times the operating profit. Every incremental gigawatt is financed, and the spread between contracted revenue yield and the blended cost of that capital is the actual business. Story 02 below is directly relevant to whether that spread widens or narrows.

The technology disclosures are worth more than they usually are. CoreWeave says it completed the industry's first bring up and validation of the NVIDIA Vera Rubin NVL72, which if it holds is a real time to market advantage on the next platform generation and the kind of thing that justifies a merchant provider existing at all. It also launched CoreWeave Interconnect, private fiber linking directly to other hyperscale platforms starting with Google Cloud, plus SUNK Anywhere and LOTA Cross Cloud. That is a strategic pivot worth noticing: CoreWeave is positioning as the high performance node inside a customer's multi cloud footprint rather than as a destination that wants to own the whole workload. It reduces switching friction in both directions, which is a bet that CoreWeave wins on performance rather than on lock in.

Skeptic's note: separate the confirmed from the modeled. Confirmed by the company release are revenue, the GAAP loss per share of 1.14 dollars, adjusted EBITDA, adjusted operating income, the interest expense line, the backlog figure with its stated caveat, and the power figures. Reported from the call and therefore secondhand are the guidance ranges and the 1.85 gigawatt year end target. Note a live discrepancy in the coverage: several outlets reported an adjusted loss per share of 1.03 dollars while others reported 1.14 dollars. The company release gives basic and diluted GAAP net loss per share of 1.14 dollars and an adjusted net loss of 567 million dollars; the 1.03 figure is a per share derivation of the adjusted number, not the GAAP result. If you are quoting a beat, be explicit about which measure you mean. Also note that two widely circulated items attached to this print, the 21 billion dollar Meta expansion and the multi year Anthropic agreement, were both announced in April 2026 and are not new this week, though several aggregators bundled them into August earnings coverage.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bullish read: the demand side of the thesis is no longer in question. A backlog of 104 billion dollars against roughly 13 billion dollars of 2026 revenue is close to eight years of coverage at the current run rate, the customer mix is broadening into industrials and enterprise software, and being first to validate Vera Rubin NVL72 suggests the technical moat is real rather than rented. The guidance raise plus the power target raise is a company saying it can convert contracts into energized megawatts faster than it previously forecast, and roughly a dozen analysts revised upward with no sell ratings on the tape. Threat read: this is now unambiguously a credit story wearing an equity costume. Interest expense at 640 million dollars is five times adjusted operating income and growing faster than revenue, adjusted operating margin compressed from 16 percent to 5 percent year over year, and the full year guide requires a second half margin materially above the first half. Backlog that is partly conditional on delivering capacity not yet built is not the same asset as contracted revenue on energized capacity. The path from here runs through the cost of capital, not through demand.

Nvidia signs memoranda with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilize more than 500 billion dollars of third party capital for compute

On Monday August 10 Nvidia announced memoranda of understanding with six of the largest alternative asset managers and banks to establish what it calls the first compute financing platforms of their kind at global scale, targeting more than 500 billion dollars of third party capital to fund GPU infrastructure for hyperscalers, frontier labs and enterprises. Jensen Huang framed the GPU as an investable asset class in accompanying interviews. Flag: these are memoranda of understanding, not binding definitive agreements, and the 500 billion dollar figure is a stated target for capital to be mobilized, not capital raised. No fund sizes, fee structures, first close dates or asset level terms have been disclosed.

Read analysis ▾

This is the most structurally important item of the week for anyone who finances compute, and it received less attention than the earnings prints because there is no number to trade against. What Nvidia is attempting is to convert GPUs from a depreciating capital expense sitting on operating balance sheets into a financeable asset class with third party capital behind it, in the way that fiber, towers and data center shells were each converted in turn.

The mechanism matters more than the headline number. If large pools of infrastructure and private credit capital will underwrite GPU fleets at infrastructure like cost of capital, then the constraint on the buildout shifts away from the balance sheets of the operators. That would be transformative for a business model whose central limitation is that GPUs depreciate on a schedule that nobody agrees on while the debt amortizes on a schedule that everybody agrees on. Nvidia has an obvious interest here: anything that lowers the financing cost of its customers increases the number of GPUs those customers can buy.

Which is also the reason to be careful. A vendor organizing the financing of its own demand is a familiar pattern and not always a benign one. Vendor facilitated financing lowers the cost of capital for buyers in the short run and concentrates correlated risk in the long run, because the same asset backs the loan, drives the revenue and sits on the vendor's order book. The telecom vendor financing episode of the late 1990s is the reference case, and the analogy is imperfect but not irrelevant. It is worth asking what happens to residual value assumptions on a five year GPU lease when the next platform generation ships on a roughly annual cadence, which is Nvidia's own stated cadence.

Skeptic's note: what is confirmed by Nvidia's own newsroom release and Blackstone's is the existence of the memoranda, the six counterparties and the more than 500 billion dollar target. Everything about structure, mechanics, timing and terms is either unstated or reported. The Financial Times broke the story before the press releases landed, and some trade coverage carried an August 11 dateline for an August 10 announcement. Treat the 500 billion dollar figure the way you would treat any announced target from a party with an interest in the number being large.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bullish read: story 01 established that CoreWeave's binding constraint is the cost and availability of capital, not demand. If Nvidia successfully institutionalizes GPU financing and drags infrastructure grade capital into the asset class, the blended cost of debt for every credible merchant operator falls and the spread that CoreWeave earns between contracted revenue and financing cost widens. CoreWeave is arguably the most direct beneficiary of GPUs becoming a recognized infrastructure asset because it is the largest pure play holder of them. Threat read: cheaper capital available to everyone is not a competitive advantage to anyone. CoreWeave's current moat includes privileged access to capital and to allocation; a platform that democratizes both invites better capitalized entrants and lets enterprises finance their own fleets rather than lease from a merchant. It also puts a formal, publicly visible price on GPU residual value, which is the assumption CoreWeave bears have been attacking without a market reference. Getting a mark is good only if the mark is good.

Nebius grows revenue 454 percent, reaches 3 billion dollars of annualized run rate, and says customers are prepaying 50 to 60 percent of the capex

Nebius reported second quarter revenue of 582.3 million dollars against 105.1 million a year earlier, growth of 454 percent, with the AI cloud business at 575 million dollars and 98 percent of the group. Adjusted EBITDA was 236.2 million dollars against a 21.0 million dollar loss a year ago. Net loss from continuing operations was 190.4 million dollars; adjusted net loss was 33.2 million dollars. Annualized run rate revenue reached 3 billion dollars at the end of June, up 598 percent year over year and 56 percent from the first quarter. Capital spending was approximately 5.66 billion dollars in the quarter against 510.6 million a year earlier. The company reaffirmed 2026 guidance of 7 to 9 billion dollars of exit annualized run rate, 3 to 3.4 billion dollars of group revenue, roughly 40 percent adjusted EBITDA margin and 20 to 25 billion dollars of capital spending. Arkady Volozh said four deals closed in the quarter each averaging more than 1 billion dollars of total contract value, with upfront payments covering 50 to 60 percent of the related capex. Flag: the reported one day share move is quoted as both roughly 16.5 percent and roughly 28 percent across outlets.

Read analysis ▾

The single most consequential sentence in the Nebius release is not a financial statement line. It is the claim that customers are paying 50 to 60 percent of the related capital expenditure upfront. If that is representative rather than cherry picked from four favourable deals, it is a materially different funding model from the one CoreWeave runs, and it is the competitive fact worth tracking.

Consider the arithmetic. Nebius spent roughly 5.66 billion dollars on capex in a quarter in which it recognized 582 million dollars of revenue. That is a ratio of nearly ten to one. A company can only sustain that if the money is coming from somewhere other than operations, and the two candidate answers are debt or customer prepayment. CoreWeave's answer is visibly debt: 640 million dollars of quarterly net interest expense. Nebius is claiming a meaningful share of the answer is customer prepayment, which is financing at a cost of capital of approximately zero and which also functions as a credible commitment device from the customer. Prepaid capacity does not get cancelled the way a signed contract with no money down does.

The counterargument is that prepayment is a buyer's market phenomenon in reverse. Customers prepay when capacity is scarce and they are worried about getting allocation. In a world where capacity is genuinely tight, prepayment terms are available to whoever has the megawatts. It is less clear that this is a durable structural advantage for Nebius as opposed to a snapshot of a scarcity regime that both companies are operating in. Watch whether the ratio holds as more capacity energizes.

The rest of the quarter is a company scaling fast and improving unit economics from a low base. Cost of revenues fell from 29 percent of revenue to 23 percent, sales and general expense from 65 percent to 30 percent, depreciation from 72 percent to 45 percent. Every operating ratio moved the right way, which is what operating leverage actually looks like on a page and is a useful contrast with story 01. But note share based compensation of 102.5 million dollars, up 597 percent and now 14 percent of total operating costs, and note that the adjusted net loss of 33.2 million dollars becomes a 190.4 million dollar GAAP loss from continuing operations once you stop adjusting. Also note the prior year comparison is distorted: Nebius posted net income of 502.5 million dollars in the second quarter of 2025 on investment gains, not operations.

Skeptic's note: revenue, adjusted EBITDA, the loss lines, the operating expense ratios and the capex figure are from the company release and are explicitly unaudited. Annualized run rate revenue is a company defined metric, not a GAAP measure, and a 3 billion dollar run rate against 582 million dollars of quarterly revenue means the exit month is running well above the quarterly average, which is a real signal but also a metric that flatters a fast ramping business. The 7 to 9 billion dollar exit run rate guidance is a wide range that has been reaffirmed rather than narrowed with two quarters left. The four deals averaging more than 1 billion dollars and the 50 to 60 percent prepayment claim come from management commentary on the call, are not broken out in the release, and the counterparties are not named.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bullish read: this is second party confirmation that merchant AI cloud demand is real, broad and not confined to one operator's customer list. Two independent companies posted triple digit growth against enormous capex in the same week, both raised or reaffirmed, and both were rewarded. Nebius at 582 million dollars of quarterly revenue is roughly a fifth of CoreWeave's scale, which supports the argument that CoreWeave's lead in energized power and platform depth is not being closed quickly. Threat read: Nebius is growing faster off a smaller base, is guiding to roughly 40 percent adjusted EBITDA margin, and is claiming a funding structure that leans on customer prepayment rather than the debt markets. If that model is real and portable, it is a structurally cheaper way to build the same business, and it will show up first in bidding for the marginal frontier lab contract. Nebius also plans 20 to 25 billion dollars of capex against CoreWeave's build, which is supply arriving into the same market. Watch whether prepayment terms start appearing in CoreWeave's contracts too; if they do not, ask why.

JLL puts first half data center absorption at 25 gigawatts, vacancy at 1 percent, and neoclouds at 11 percent of demand

JLL published its North America Data Center Report for midyear 2026 on Tuesday August 11. Headline figures: 25 gigawatts of absorption in the first half, roughly double the year earlier period and about five times the level of two years ago; vacancy at 1 percent for a third consecutive year; 66 gigawatts under construction with 95 percent already pre committed; Texas at 26 gigawatts and Virginia at 13 gigawatts; frontier markets at 77 percent of the pipeline. On financing, JLL expects more than 700 billion dollars of permanent debt originations over the next 30 months, counts 250 billion dollars of AI related bond issuance in the first half split between 218 billion investment grade and 32 billion high yield, and 17 billion dollars of commercial mortgage backed and asset backed issuance, up 29 percent. Demand mix: hyperscalers 59 percent, neoclouds 11 percent, pure play AI companies 7 percent. Flag: these are JLL proprietary research estimates, not audited or official statistics, and JLL is a brokerage with a commercial interest in the sector it measures.

Read analysis ▾

This is the best hard dataset published in the window and it is the one to keep. Two figures reframe how you should think about competitive positioning.

The first is 95 percent of 66 gigawatts under construction already pre committed against 1 percent vacancy. There is effectively no speculative capacity in North America. Every megawatt being built has a name on it before it energizes. That means the competition between merchant providers is not a competition for tenants in a market with slack; it is a competition for the right to build, which resolves into land, power interconnection and financing rather than into sales. It also means the market has almost no shock absorber. A demand pause does not show up as rising vacancy for a year or two, because there is no vacancy; it shows up immediately as cancelled pre commitments and stranded construction.

The second is the demand mix: hyperscalers 59 percent, neoclouds 11 percent, pure play AI 7 percent, with 28 gigawatts of hyperscaler owner occupied capacity under construction. The neocloud category, which is CoreWeave's category, is roughly one ninth of the demand. Set that against the fact that hyperscalers are building 28 gigawatts for themselves. The merchant compute thesis rests on the claim that hyperscalers will keep outsourcing a meaningful slice of AI capacity rather than internalizing it, and this dataset shows the internalization is running at large scale in parallel. That is not a refutation, because CoreWeave's largest customers are hyperscalers and labs who do both. But an 11 percent share is a reminder that merchant compute is a specialized segment of a market that is mostly building for itself.

The financing figures deserve their own note in light of story 02. Two hundred and fifty billion dollars of AI related bond issuance in a single half, with 218 billion of it investment grade, plus more than 700 billion dollars of expected permanent debt originations over 30 months, is the debt market already doing at scale what Nvidia's six manager platform is proposing to do in equity like private capital. The AI buildout is being funded predominantly by investment grade bonds issued by investment grade issuers. The relevant question for a non investment grade merchant operator is what share of that 700 billion dollars is accessible at a rate that clears its own return hurdle.

Skeptic's note: every figure here is JLL's own research using JLL's own market definitions. Absorption, pre commitment and frontier market are all defined by the author. The 5C financing in Quick Signals below and the 250 billion dollar bond figure are consistent with a market awash in credit, but a brokerage publishing bullish absorption data about the market it brokers is not a neutral observer. Cross check the direction of travel, not the decimal places.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bullish read: 1 percent vacancy and 95 percent pre commitment is the empirical version of CoreWeave's pricing power argument. In a market with no slack, the operator with 1.5 gigawatts energized and 3.7 gigawatts contracted holds a scarce and appreciating position, and the 700 billion dollar debt origination forecast says the capital to keep building will be there. Threat read: neoclouds are 11 percent of demand while hyperscalers build 28 gigawatts for their own account, which frames merchant compute as a supplementary channel rather than the main artery. Zero vacancy also cuts both ways; it means there is no buffer, and any demand air pocket transmits straight into cancelled commitments and stranded construction, which is precisely the scenario in which a 104 billion dollar conditional backlog gets repriced.

BloombergNEF says the Texas interconnection pause puts roughly 20 percent of the US data center pipeline at risk, and cuts its 2027 ERCOT demand growth forecast from 14 percent to 6 percent

BloombergNEF analysis published Tuesday August 11 estimates that approximately 49.8 gigawatts of projects seeking grid connection in Texas are affected by the state's review of data center interconnection requests, representing roughly a fifth of the national pipeline, with potential financial impact above 8 billion dollars by the first quarter of 2027. BNEF cut its ERCOT 2027 power demand growth forecast to 6 percent from 14 percent in July. The ERCOT interconnection queue exceeds 470 gigawatts, of which roughly 90 percent is data center load, and BNEF still expects only about 8.25 gigawatts of incremental data center capacity to actually come online in ERCOT through 2030. Flag: these are BNEF estimates, not official ERCOT or PUCT figures. Flag on dating: the analysis is new this week, but the underlying trigger, the Texas governor's directive to audit interconnection queue data centers, dates to early August and predates this window.

Read analysis ▾

Read this next to story 04 and a picture forms. JLL counts 26 gigawatts under construction in Texas, the largest single market in North America. BNEF counts roughly 470 gigawatts sitting in the ERCOT interconnection queue, about 90 percent of it data centers, and expects roughly 8.25 gigawatts of it to actually energize through 2030. Those two numbers are not in conflict, they are describing different stages of the same funnel, and the gap between them is the story. Queue position is not capacity. The queue is now so oversubscribed relative to what the grid can absorb that it has stopped functioning as a signal of anything except optimism, which is why the state started auditing it.

The mechanical risk is straightforward. A project that cannot get an interconnection date cannot be financed on a timeline, and a project that cannot be financed on a timeline cannot support a pre commitment. In a market where 95 percent of construction is pre committed, an interconnection pause propagates backwards into the contracts. The 8 billion dollar figure by the first quarter of 2027 is BNEF's estimate of what that delay costs.

The broader read is that the binding constraint on the AI buildout has moved decisively from chips to electrons, and from electrons to permission. Monday's edition covered the political backlash forming around a 7.65 gigawatt gas plant in West Texas and the data center siting fight becoming a bipartisan election issue. This is the regulatory expression of the same pressure, arriving faster than expected in the most permissive large market in the country. If Texas is where the queue gets audited, the assumption that other states are more forgiving needs testing rather than assuming.

Skeptic's note: every quantitative claim here belongs to BloombergNEF. The 49.8 gigawatt figure, the roughly 20 percent share of national pipeline, the 8 billion dollar impact and the revised 6 percent growth forecast are analyst estimates using BNEF's own project database and assumptions. The ERCOT queue size is closer to an official figure but queue data is notoriously noisy because developers file speculatively and in multiple locations. The forecast cut from 14 percent to 6 percent is a large revision in a single month, which should make you ask how much confidence to place in either number. A pause is also not a cancellation; the review could clear projects quickly.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bullish read: CoreWeave already holds approximately 3.7 gigawatts of contracted power and 1.5 gigawatts energized. Anything that makes new interconnection harder increases the scarcity value of secured power, and a company that has already done the hard part benefits when the hard part gets harder for everyone else. Speed to power is the moat, and a regulatory bottleneck deepens it. Threat read: CoreWeave has to keep growing, and the year end target of more than 1.85 gigawatts of active power plus everything behind the 104 billion dollar backlog requires energizing capacity that does not yet exist. If Texas is representative, the cost and timeline of every future megawatt rises, which pressures the delivery schedule that the backlog is explicitly conditioned on. It also raises a diligence question worth asking internally: how much of the contracted 3.7 gigawatts sits in ERCOT, and how much of that has a firm interconnection date rather than a queue position.

OpenAI ships GPT-5.6-Cyber, a model deliberately trained to refuse less on dual use offensive security work

On Monday August 10 OpenAI published "Expanding Daybreak as the Cyber Defense Window Narrows" and released GPT-5.6-Cyber, splitting its Daybreak cyber program into Blue and Red tiers. OpenAI reports the model completes 95 percent of advanced dual use requests covering exploit chains, authentication bypass and privilege escalation, against 1.5 percent for GPT-5.6 Sol, 2 percent via Daybreak Blue and 57.3 percent for GPT-5.5-Cyber. OpenAI self assesses the model as High rather than Critical on its own Preparedness Framework. Named partners incorporating it include Accenture, IBM, CrowdStrike, Cisco and Palo Alto Networks. Reported pricing is 12.50 dollars per million input tokens, 75 dollars per million output and 1.25 dollars per million cached. Flag: every benchmark figure is OpenAI internal and self reported, and as of this edition no system card had been published, so none of it is externally checkable.

Read analysis ▾

Monday's edition led with OpenAI pausing parts of its Astra work because internal evaluations could not rule out a critical cybersecurity capability level. This is the other half of the same posture, published three days later, and the pair is more informative than either alone. OpenAI is simultaneously withholding a general model it cannot bound and shipping a specialized model explicitly tuned to do the thing it was worried about, to a gated set of security vendors. The organizing logic is that the capability is arriving regardless and the defenders should get it first under access control.

That is a defensible position and it is also the most consequential deployment decision a frontier lab has made this year. The load bearing assumption is that gated distribution to named enterprise partners actually holds. A 95 percent completion rate on exploit chain requests is, by construction, an offensive capability. The mitigation is entirely access control, and access control on API distributed models has a mixed record.

The economics are worth a moment. At 75 dollars per million output tokens, this is priced at a large premium to general purpose frontier inference. Specialized high value models with restricted distribution and premium pricing are a different revenue shape from commodity token serving, and if this becomes a pattern it changes what the inference market looks like: fewer tokens, higher value, more compliance overhead, more dedicated capacity.

Skeptic's note: this is the least externally verified major item in the edition. The pricing figures come from documentation reported secondhand. The partner list is from OpenAI's announcement and does not tell you the scope of any partner's actual deployment.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Bullish read: specialized, expensive, access controlled models running for regulated security vendors are exactly the workload profile that favours dedicated infrastructure over shared public cloud. If frontier labs move toward a portfolio of narrow high value models with distinct isolation and compliance requirements rather than one general model serving everyone, the demand for segregated, auditable, high performance capacity rises, and merchant providers with sovereign and classified experience are positioned for it. Threat read: two indirect risks. First, if offensive capability at this level proliferates, infrastructure operators become higher value targets and the security and compliance cost of running frontier workloads rises for everyone hosting them. Second, and more strategically, a lab that pauses one model and ships another based on unpublished internal evaluations is demonstrating that deployment decisions with direct revenue consequences for its infrastructure providers can be made unilaterally and without notice. Capacity planning against frontier lab demand carries that policy risk.
