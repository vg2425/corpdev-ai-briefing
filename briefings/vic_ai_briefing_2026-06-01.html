Anthropic raises $65B Series H at a $965B valuation, passing OpenAI as the most valuable AI startup and setting up an IPO as soon as October

Anthropic announced its Series H on May 28, led by Altimeter, Dragoneer, Greenoaks and Sequoia, at a $965B post money valuation. That edges past OpenAI's $852B March round, and reporting describes this as likely the last private raise before a public listing that could come as early as October 2026.

▶

The numbers. $65B raised at $965B post money, up from $183B at the Series G in February, a 5.3x step in roughly eight months. Anthropic's own announcement and TechCrunch both cite run rate revenue crossing $47B earlier in May, which is the figure that makes the valuation defensible at roughly 20x run rate revenue rather than the much higher multiples the company carried a year ago. Stated use of funds is the usual triad: safety and interpretability research, compute expansion for Claude, and scaling products and partnerships.

The competitive framing is the part worth carrying. Anthropic is now the most valuable private AI company, ahead of OpenAI, and the gap between "model lab" and "trillion dollar enterprise software company" has effectively closed in the private markets. I would push back gently on the headline excitement: a $965B private mark set by growth and crossover investors in a momentum round is not the same as a public market clearing price, and the October IPO timing is reported as a possibility, not a commitment. Treat the $47B run rate as the number to interrogate, since revenue durability, not valuation, is what the IPO will be priced on.

Cross source check: Anthropic's own Series H post, TechCrunch, CNBC, Yahoo Finance, Alternatives Watch, Funds for NGOs, indmoney and several others converge on the $65B raise, $965B valuation, the named lead investors, and the "passes OpenAI" framing. The October 2026 IPO window is reported by multiple outlets but is characterized as under consideration rather than scheduled.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Anthropic is one of the most important demand signals in CoreWeave's universe, and a $65B raise earmarked partly for "compute expansion for Claude" is a direct read on future inference and training demand. The strategic nuance: Anthropic's stated infrastructure posture spreads workloads across AWS Trainium, Google TPU and Nvidia GPUs, so the question for CoreWeave is share of that expanding pie rather than whether the pie grows. An Anthropic IPO would also force public disclosure of compute commitments and supplier concentration, which would give CoreWeave and its investors the cleanest external benchmark yet for how frontier inference economics actually flow through to GPU cloud providers. Watch the S-1, if it comes, for named infrastructure counterparties.

Jensen Huang opens COMPUTEX with Nemotron 3 Ultra, a 550B open weights model built for agents, plus Vera CPU and an annual Spectrum-X cadence

At the GTC Taipei keynote at COMPUTEX on June 1, Nvidia unveiled Nemotron 3 Ultra, its largest open weights model at 550B parameters with 55B active, positioned as the leading US open model for agentic workloads. Huang also pushed the Vera CPU and committed to launching new Spectrum-X Ethernet products every year.

▶

Model specifics. Nemotron 3 Ultra uses a hybrid Mamba Transformer mixture of experts design, 550B total parameters with roughly 55B active per token, trained in NVFP4. Nvidia and Artificial Analysis cite an Intelligence Index around 48 and throughput north of 300 tokens per second, which would make it the strongest US open weights model on those particular measures. I would caveat both numbers: the Intelligence Index figure is Nvidia and Artificial Analysis sourced and the throughput claim is hardware dependent, so treat them as vendor framing until independent reproduction lands.

The strategic story is Nvidia continuing to climb the stack. A frontier scale open weights model from the company that sells the GPUs is a deliberate move to seed agentic demand that runs best on Nvidia silicon, while keeping the model itself free to undercut closed model pricing. Pair that with the Vera CPU (Nvidia cites 1.8x faster task completion for agent workloads) and the commitment to annual Spectrum-X Ethernet refreshes, and the throughline is Nvidia trying to own the full agentic stack from networking to CPU to GPU to the model. Huang also announced an RTX Spark laptop class chip, which extends the same agentic framing down to the edge.

Cross source check: Nvidia Newsroom, Artificial Analysis, Crypto Briefing, KuCoin, Phemex, Coinpedia, explainx.ai and Seeking Alpha all confirm the Nemotron 3 Ultra launch, the 500 to 550B parameter framing, the agentic positioning, and the Vera and Spectrum-X announcements. The exact active parameter count (55B) and Intelligence Index (48) appear in the Artificial Analysis and Nvidia writeups specifically.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Two direct reads. First, a free 550B open weights model optimized for agents expands the population of high intensity inference workloads that need to run somewhere, and open weights models are exactly the kind of workload that lands on neoclouds rather than locked inside a hyperscaler's first party API. That is structurally good for CoreWeave's inference book. Second, the Vera CPU and annual Spectrum-X cadence reinforce the point from last week's Nvidia print: the reference architecture for Rubin class deployments is shifting toward an all Nvidia bill of materials, which tightens performance and lock in but increases single vendor concentration in CoreWeave's supply chain. Worth tracking whether CoreWeave positions itself as a launch host for Nemotron 3 Ultra inference, which would be a clean marketing and demand win.

🔥 High Signal Models Agents 9 sources

Anthropic ships Claude Opus 4.8 with Dynamic Workflows, letting Claude Code orchestrate up to 1,000 parallel subagents

Released May 28 at the same price as Opus 4.7, Opus 4.8 lands alongside Dynamic Workflows, a feature that lets Claude Code plan a large job, spin up subagents (16 concurrent, 1,000 total per run), and verify each result against a test suite with no manual orchestration. A cheaper fast mode also now supports Opus 4.8.

▶

What shipped. Opus 4.8 is available immediately across the Claude API, Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud Vertex AI and Microsoft Foundry. Reported benchmarks include 88.6 percent on SWE-bench Verified and 74.6 percent on Terminal-Bench 2.1, with marketing emphasis on being roughly 4x more honest about its own code bugs. I would flag the honesty and benchmark figures as Anthropic sourced and not yet independently reproduced. The pricing held flat at the Opus 4.7 level, with fast mode offering a cheaper tier.

Dynamic Workflows is the more strategically interesting piece. Rather than a human chaining subagents, Claude writes a JavaScript orchestration script for the task you describe, and a runtime executes it in the background, fanning out to as many as 1,000 subagents per run with 16 running concurrently. The framing target is repository scale migrations and other large mechanical engineering jobs. The honest read is that this is the same direction the whole field is moving (background, long horizon, unsupervised agent loops), and the differentiation is in reliability and verification rather than the headline subagent count. The 1,000 number is a ceiling, not a typical workload.

Cross source check: TechCrunch, MarkTechPost, MindStudio, Appwrite, AI Weekly, Digital Applied and several others align on the May 28 release, the 1,000 subagent cap with 16 concurrent, multi cloud availability at launch, and flat pricing versus Opus 4.7. The specific benchmark scores vary slightly across secondary coverage and originate with Anthropic.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Coding agents remain the most inference intensive workload at the frontier, and Dynamic Workflows is explicitly designed to multiply tokens per task by fanning a single request into hundreds of parallel subagent calls. That is the workload profile CoreWeave wants more of: bursty, high concurrency, latency tolerant inference that fills fleet capacity. The detail to underline is that Opus 4.8 launched simultaneously on Bedrock, Vertex and Microsoft Foundry, which is the recurring pattern where Anthropic's largest inference volumes route through hyperscaler contracts. For CoreWeave the route to this demand runs largely through its AWS and Microsoft relationships rather than direct, so the relevant metric is fleet utilization driven by agentic burst, not logo level customer wins.

Agents Models 7 sources

Google's Gemini Spark goes live May 29 for US Ultra subscribers, an always on agent that persists on Google Cloud VMs after you close the tab

Previewed at I/O on May 19, Gemini Spark rolled out May 29 to US Google AI Ultra subscribers at the newly reduced $100 per month tier. It runs on Gemini 3.5 Flash plus the Antigravity 2.0 agent harness, and unlike the standard chatbot it persists on dedicated Google Cloud VMs, continuing to work when your phone is locked or laptop is shut.

▶

What it is. Spark is Google's personal agent play: a system that reasons across your connected apps (Gmail integration is the headline), takes long horizon actions on your behalf, and runs in the background on persistent cloud infrastructure rather than ending when you close the tab. Pichai framed it as the next evolution of the digital assistant. Access is US only at launch on the Ultra tier, with EU and UK availability gated on AI Act compliance review and analysts expecting Q3 2026 at the earliest.

The architecturally interesting detail for an infrastructure audience is that Spark runs on dedicated Google Cloud virtual machines that persist per user. That is a different compute profile from request and response chat: it is closer to always on, per user allocated capacity, which is far more expensive to serve and explains why it sits behind the top subscription tier. The honest caveat is that "runs all day in the background" is a capability claim that will be tested by real usage; persistent agents have a habit of being throttled hard once the compute bill becomes visible.

Cross source check: 9to5Google confirms the May 29 US Ultra rollout, with the I/O preview covered by TechCrunch, CNBC, The Next Web and others on May 19. The persistent Google Cloud VM detail and the $100 Ultra tier appear in the 9to5Google and DataCamp writeups.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Spark is a clean illustration of where agentic compute is heading: away from stateless chat and toward persistent, per user, always on allocations that consume capacity even when idle. That profile is structurally more compute hungry than chat and is the demand shape that benefits GPU and accelerator capacity providers broadly. The CoreWeave specific caveat is that Spark runs on Google's own TPU stack inside Google Cloud, so it does not touch CoreWeave directly. The read across is competitive intelligence: as OpenAI and Anthropic ship their own persistent agents, the ones that run on Nvidia GPUs are the ones that flow to neoclouds. Watch whether the persistent agent pattern becomes the default product shape, because it changes capacity planning from peak concurrent sessions to allocated seats.

Infra Business 8 sources

GitHub Copilot moves all plans to usage based billing on June 1, pricing agentic sessions by token consumption

Effective June 1, every GitHub Copilot plan transitions to usage based billing built around a monthly allotment of GitHub AI Credits, with extra usage metered by token consumption at API rates. Base subscription prices hold (Pro $10, Pro+ $39, Business and Enterprise $19 and $39), but heavy agentic users will pay materially more.

▶

What changed. GitHub's stated rationale is blunt: Copilot now powers agentic workflows that consume far more compute than a year ago, a quick chat and a multi hour autonomous coding session cost GitHub very differently, and the flat premium request model is no longer sustainable. Each plan now includes a credit allotment, paid plans can buy more, and usage is metered on input, output and cached tokens at listed model API rates. Code completions and next edit suggestions remain unbilled and unlimited on paid plans, which protects the original Copilot use case.

This is one of the clearest public admissions yet that agentic inference economics break flat rate SaaS pricing. Developer reaction, per Visual Studio Magazine, has been negative, with the common complaint being "you will get less but pay the same" for heavy users. The honest framing is that this is the inevitable repricing of AI software from per seat to per consumption, and Copilot is simply early and visible because of its scale. Expect every agent priced product to follow, because nobody can subsidize unbounded background token burn indefinitely.

Cross source check: GitHub's own blog and changelog, GitHub community discussion, Visual Studio Magazine, Enterprise DNA, Windows Forum and how2shout all confirm the June 1 effective date, the AI Credits structure, the unchanged base prices and the metered token model. The developer backlash framing is reported across Visual Studio Magazine and how2shout.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

This is a leading indicator for the entire inference value chain that CoreWeave sits under. When the largest developer AI product publicly concedes that flat pricing cannot cover agentic token burn, it validates the thesis that inference demand (and therefore the compute underneath it) is growing faster than per seat revenue, which forces the whole stack toward consumption pricing. For CoreWeave that is directionally positive on volume: more metered agentic usage means more raw inference, which is the workload that fills GPU fleets. The risk embedded in the same trend is price compression at the model layer eventually squeezing what infrastructure providers can charge, so the number to watch is whether token volume growth keeps outrunning per token price declines.

Policy Business 6 sources

OpenAI publishes a Frontier Governance Framework mapping its safety practices to the EU AI Act and California's frontier law

On May 28 to 29, OpenAI released a Frontier Governance Framework that translates its existing Preparedness Framework into a public governance document aligned with the EU AI Act's Code of Practice for General Purpose AI and California's Transparency in Frontier AI Act. It is a regulatory translation layer, not a new safety methodology.

▶

What it is. The document maps OpenAI's internal risk work across cyber offense, CBRN, harmful manipulation and loss of control onto specific external regulatory obligations: model reporting, security risk management, incident response, external expert input and framework update cadence. OpenAI is explicit that the Preparedness Framework remains the substantive foundation and the new document is about demonstrating compliance with emerging law rather than changing how risk is assessed.

The timing is the signal. This lands the same week as OpenAI's IPO positioning and the broader regulatory churn (Tech Times notes parallel fronts including a CNN suit against Perplexity and EU rule alignment). The defensible read is that frontier labs are racing to show regulators a clean compliance story before listing and before the EU AI Act's general purpose provisions bite. The more skeptical read is that a "regulatory translation layer" is exactly the kind of document that is cheap to produce and signals cooperation without constraining behavior. Both readings can be true at once.

Cross source check: OpenAI's own index page and PDF, StartupHub, keepingupwith.ai, CIO Dive, Artificial Intelligence News and Tech Times confirm the framework, the EU AI Act and California alignment, and the May 28 to 29 timing. Some outlets date it May 28 and some May 29, a minor discrepancy worth noting.

Why It Matters for CoreWeave

Indirect but worth carrying. As frontier labs formalize compliance frameworks tied to the EU AI Act and state law, the obligations propagate down the stack to infrastructure providers through contractual flow downs: data residency, incident reporting, security controls and audit rights. CoreWeave increasingly needs to be able to demonstrate that its infrastructure supports customer compliance with these regimes, particularly for EU resident workloads. The competitive angle is that "compliance ready infrastructure" is becoming a procurement criterion, and a neocloud that can credibly support EU AI Act obligations has an edge in winning regulated enterprise and lab workloads against less prepared competitors.
